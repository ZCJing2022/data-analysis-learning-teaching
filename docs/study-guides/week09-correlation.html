Week 09 — Correlation

Overview

Weeks 6–8 asked whether a mean or a set of group means differed from what we would expect under a null hypothesis. Week 9 changes the question. Instead of comparing conditions, we ask whether two measured variables vary together. Do students who study longer tend to earn higher grades? Do higher stress scores tend to accompany shorter sleep durations? How can we describe such patterns, and how much evidence is there that the association extends beyond the sample?

The lecture’s sequence (Sigal, 2026, slides 3–43) is:

Paired observations → Scatterplots → Covariance → Pearson’s r → Hypothesis testing → Uses and limitations → Effect size → Rank and binary correlations → Interpretation and prediction.

The required reading, Privitera (2018, Chapter 13, Sections 13.1–13.7 and the correlation-reporting material in Section 13.13), supports this sequence. Privitera (2024, Chapter 15) deepens the discussion of assumptions and alternative coefficients. Regression in the latter half of the 2018 chapter is introduced briefly here as a bridge to Week 10, not as an additional Week 9 calculation requirement. The worksheet and GTA VI formula walkthrough help connect concepts to calculations.

NoteThe central distinction

A paired t test analyzes the mean of differences between two comparable measurements. A correlation analyzes the pattern of co-variation between paired observations, which may have entirely different measurement units. Neither the presence of paired data nor a statistically significant correlation establishes a causal effect.

Learning Objectives

By the end of Week 9, you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish correlational research from the group-comparison designs of earlier weeks and organize bivariate data correctly.
  2. Read a scatterplot for direction, strength, form, unusual points, and range; explain regression toward the mean.
  3. Calculate deviations, sums of squares, sum of products, and sample covariance; interpret the sign of cross-products.
  4. Explain why covariance depends on measurement units and why standardization produces a unitless Pearson coefficient.
  5. Compute Pearson’s r using covariance, the sum-of-products formula, or standardized scores; explain its range and symmetry.
  6. State population hypotheses and use Appendix C.5 with \(df=n-2\), the chosen alpha and number of tails; distinguish significance from strength.
  7. Explain the applications of correlation in prediction, validity, theory evaluation and reliability.
  8. Evaluate linearity, homoscedasticity, normality, independence and the measurement scale; recognize outliers and restriction of range.
  9. Distinguish association from causation, including reverse causality, reciprocal effects, third variables and spurious relationships.
  10. Interpret r as an effect size and calculate and interpret the coefficient of determination.
  11. Calculate Spearman’s rank correlation, including average ranks for ties, and use Appendix C.6 with the sample size.
  12. Select and compute point-biserial and phi coefficients using 0/1 coding and connect them to t and chi-square statistics.
  13. Report correlations clearly, including sample size, direction, strength, significance and interpretive limits; connect correlation with regression.

Part I — From Comparing Means to Measuring Relationships

1. What Carries Over from Weeks 6–8?

A mean-comparison test starts with a benchmark or a grouping variable and asks whether the outcome differs. A correlation starts with two measurements for each observational unit and asks how the measurements co-vary. In a study of texting and performance, comparing exam grades of students who text versus those who do not gives a two-group comparison. Counting texts and recording a grade for each student gives a quantitative association question (Privitera, 2018, Figure 13.1; 2024, Figure 15.1).

Research question Statistical object Method
Does one sample mean differ from a known benchmark? Mean departure One-sample z or t test
Do two unrelated groups have different means? Independent mean difference Independent-samples t test
Do the same participants change from pre to post? Mean paired difference Paired t test
Do three independent groups differ in their means? Between-group versus within-group mean squares One-way ANOVA
Do two quantitative measurements tend to vary together? Standardized co-variation Pearson correlation

The accompanying GTA VI Formula Walkthrough (pp. 1–4) encourages students to identify the numerator and denominator separately. For a z or t test, the numerator is a departure from a null mean and the denominator is a standard error. For ANOVA, the numerator and denominator are mean squares. For Pearson’s r, the numerator is a sum of cross-products (or covariance) and the denominator scales that quantity by the spread of both variables. It is not a standard error. Its maximum magnitude is one; z, t and F do not share that bound.

The walkthrough’s hypothetical commitment-scale examples reinforce test selection: \(z=2.00\) with known population SD; \(t(24)=2.00\) with estimated SD; paired \(t(15)=3.00\); pooled independent \(t(38)=5.66\); and an illustrative \(F(2,42)=8.00\) from supplied mean squares. At two-tailed .05 the z example is significant but the one-sample t example is not (critical t = 2.064). The ANOVA example’s mean squares are hypothetical inputs, not derived from its stated group means: those means (65, 52, 49; 15 per group) actually imply \(MS_{between}=1085\). Under the F-test null, both mean squares estimate the same variance, so the reference scale is around 1, not 0. The handout’s magnitude ranges for t and F are checking heuristics, not mathematical bounds.

Use the handout’s three-pass method: mark the observations or effect ingredients first, identify spread and sample-size ingredients second, and eliminate irrelevant numbers third. Compute intermediate quantities separately, then combine them. Translate each result into a sentence before deciding what it means.

2. Correlational Research in Psychology

The lecture refers to Lee Cronbach’s distinction between experimental and correlational traditions. Experimental research emphasizes manipulating conditions under control; correlational research often emphasizes naturally occurring differences between people and their relationships with other measured characteristics. The statistical coefficient is not restricted to observational studies, but the design determines what conclusions we can draw.

Pairs may be measurements within one person (sleep and attention), measurements on related people (parent and child scores), or a personal and environmental measure (stress and neighborhood noise). Each row must have a meaningful pair, and independent rows are needed for ordinary inference. Multiple observations on the same person or participants in the same household can create dependence across rows.

The lecture’s scenarios (slide 5) illustrate plausible hypotheses rather than established results: income and education might show a positive pattern; sugar and energy could be nonlinear; beer and temperature might increase together while hot coffee sales could decrease; car age and resale value might show a negative pattern; coffee intake and intelligence might show little linear association. Only observed data can establish the pattern.

3. Scatterplots: One Point per Pair

A scatterplot places each pair \((x_i,y_i)\) at the intersection of its X and Y values. Never sort X and Y independently before plotting or computing correlation: that destroys the original pairing. The two axes need not use the same units. Read the axis labels and scales before judging the shape.

Examine four features: direction (upward, downward, or neither); strength (how tightly points follow a straight-line pattern relative to their spread); form (straight, curved, clustered); and unusual observations or restricted ranges. The ellipse drawn in the lecture annotations is a useful mental picture for roughly elliptical clouds: orientation describes direction and narrowness describes consistency. It is not a formal calculation, and aspect ratio can change the visual impression of steepness. A steep line does not automatically mean a strong correlation.

4. Galton’s Peas and Regression Toward the Mean

Slides 6–9 introduce the historical work on parent and offspring seed size. The original graph on slide 8 is labeled 1877 and shows an upward relationship: larger parent seeds generally produce larger offspring seeds, but the offspring of extreme parents tend to be less extreme. The lecture associates these observations with the historical development of correlation by Galton and Pearson; Pearson’s later formal coefficient should not be confused with the historical graph’s notation.

Regression toward the mean describes an average tendency, not a rule for each individual. In standardized units, when a positive linear association is imperfect, the least-squares prediction of Y for an extreme X is less extreme than X: \(\widehat z_Y=r z_X\). For example, if \(r=.50\) and a parent’s standardized value is 2, the predicted offspring value is 1. This does not mean every offspring is smaller, or that an intervention caused improvement. Selecting students with unusually low first-test scores can likewise yield less extreme retest scores even without a treatment effect.

5. Direction and Strength of Pearson’s r

Pearson’s sample coefficient is denoted \(r\); the population coefficient is \(\rho\) (rho). When both variables have nonzero spread, \(-1\le r\le 1\). The sign is direction, and \(|r|\) is strength. A correlation of −.80 is stronger than +.05, and +.80 and −.80 have equal magnitude. At ±1, all points lie exactly on a nonhorizontal, nonvertical straight line. At zero, there is no linear association in the sample; there can still be a strong nonlinear pattern.

The lecture’s age-by-CARS example (slides 25–26) reports \(r=+.229\). Its mean lines divide the graph into four quadrants, with points distributed in all four. A weak positive linear relationship is visible, not a perfectly consistent increase. The full paired ages and CARS scores are not provided as a table, so we use the reported coefficient without reconstructing exact data from the graph. The following sections explain why the balance of deviations matters.

Part II — From Variance to Covariance and Pearson’s r

6. Variance Revisited

Recall from Week 3 that sample variance measures spread around the mean:

\[ SS_X=\sum (x_i-\bar{x})^2,\qquad s_X^2=\frac{SS_X}{n-1}. \]

Each deviation measures distance and direction relative to the mean. Squaring makes every term nonnegative, because we want spread rather than cancellation. With two variables, we instead ask whether deviations from their respective means tend to have the same or opposite signs.

7. Covariance and the Sum of Products

\[ SP=\sum (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y}),\qquad \operatorname{Cov}_{X,Y}=\frac{SP}{n-1}. \]

The textbook also writes \(SS_{XY}\) for SP. Despite that notation, it is a sum of cross-products, not a sum of nonnegative squares. A point above both means or below both means contributes positively; a point above one mean and below the other contributes negatively. A point on either mean line contributes zero.

X deviation Y deviation Product
Positive Positive Positive
Negative Negative Positive
Positive Negative Negative
Negative Positive Negative

Covariance is the sample average cross-product in the degrees-of-freedom-adjusted sense. The numerator sums signed products, so positive and negative contributions may cancel. The magnitude of a product also matters: counting dots in quadrants alone is not enough. Covariance of X with itself is the variance of X.

8. Why Standardize Covariance?

Covariance depends on both measurement scales. If X is in hours and Y in points, covariance is in hour-points. Converting X to minutes multiplies its covariance with Y by 60. If both variables in miles are converted to kilometers, the covariance is multiplied by approximately \(1.609^2\) (the lecture’s 4.25 becomes approximately 11.0). A larger number after a change of units does not indicate a stronger relationship.

The lecture removes units by dividing covariance by the product of the two standard deviations:

\[ r=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_{X,Y}}{s_Xs_Y}. \]

Because \(s_X=\sqrt{SS_X/(n-1)}\) and similarly for Y, the common divisor cancels:

\[ r=\frac{SP}{\sqrt{SS_XSS_Y}}. \]

This is the second formula in lecture slide 14 and the main computational form in Privitera (2018, Section 13.3). The denominator is a product of spreads, not their sum, and is positive if both variables vary. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, \(|SP|\le\sqrt{SS_XSS_Y}\), which explains the bound on r. Covariance itself is not bounded by ±1 and is not a proportion of variance.

A third equivalent expression uses Week 4 sample standardized scores:

\[ z_{Xi}=\frac{x_i-\bar{x}}{s_X},\qquad z_{Yi}=\frac{y_i-\bar{y}}{s_Y},\qquad r=\frac{\sum z_{Xi}z_{Yi}}{n-1}. \]

These are products, not differences. Standardizing both columns allows us to read r as average standardized co-variation. Do not divide by n when using sample-standardized scores.

Method 1 — covariance:

\[ \operatorname{Cov}_{X,Y}=\frac{16.5}{3}=5.5,\quad s_X=\sqrt{8.75/3}=1.7078,\quad s_Y=\sqrt{33/3}=3.3166. \]

\[ r=\frac{16.5}{\sqrt{8.75(33)}}=+.971008. \]

Both calculations give the same result. The positive sign is supported by all four positive products. The points align closely but not perfectly along an ascending line. With only four pairs, a strong sample correlation does not automatically establish population significance; that is the next task.

TipCalculation checklist

Keep the data paired. Compute both means. Calculate each signed deviation and its cross-product. Verify that both deviation columns sum to zero (up to rounding). Sum the products and the squared deviations separately, then take the square root of the product of the sums of squares. Round only at the end. If the result is outside −1 to +1, look for an arithmetic or formula error.

10. Unit Changes, Reversal, and Special Cases

Adding or subtracting a constant from either variable leaves the deviations unchanged. Multiplying either variable by a positive constant changes the numerator and denominator by the same factor, so r stays unchanged. Multiplying exactly one variable by a negative constant reverses the sign without changing its magnitude. Reversing both leaves r unchanged. A nonlinear transformation can change Pearson’s r, even when the rank ordering is unchanged.

Correlation is symmetric: \(r_{XY}=r_{YX}\). A slope is not symmetric and depends on units; we will distinguish these in Week 10.

If every X value is identical, \(SS_X=0\), and r has a zero denominator. It is undefined, not zero. This corrects the constant-score illustration in Privitera (2018, pp. 727–728; 2024, p. 600). There must be variation in both variables before a correlation is meaningful.

Part III — Testing and Interpreting Pearson’s Correlation

11. The Four Steps of Hypothesis Testing

A sample correlation can be nonzero because of sampling variability. To test whether the population linear correlation differs from zero, follow the same four-step approach used in Week 6.

  1. Hypotheses: \(H_0:\rho=0\) and \(H_1:\rho\ne0\) for a nondirectional test. Use population rho, not sample r, in the hypotheses. A directional test requires a justified prediction fixed before seeing the data.
  2. Criteria: choose alpha and tails, and use \(df=n-2\) in Appendix C.5 (2018 edition). Here n counts complete pairs, not the number of individual values in both columns. The 2024 edition uses Appendix D.5.
  3. Statistic: compute r from paired data (or use the provided coefficient).
  4. Decision: for a two-tailed test, reject when \(|r|\ge r_{crit}\) using the appropriate table precision. Otherwise fail to reject. For a one-tailed test, the sign must also match the prespecified alternative.

A p value is the probability under the null model of a statistic at least as extreme in the relevant tail(s). It is not the probability the null is true. A nonsignificant result does not prove zero association. When an obtained value is near a rounded critical value, retain precision or use the exact p value.

As the sample size decreases, a larger absolute correlation is needed to reject the null at the same alpha. Small samples fluctuate more; even a large observed association can arise under the null. The significance of r depends on its magnitude, n, alpha and the chosen tails. For example, a correlation of .60 at n = 10 does not reach .05 (critical .632), whereas .60 at n = 25 does (critical .396). A smaller alpha generally requires a larger magnitude.

For optional software verification, the equivalent t test for zero Pearson correlation is

\[ t=r\sqrt{\frac{n-2}{1-r^2}},\qquad df=n-2. \]

This t transformation is useful for computing an exact parametric p value, but the lecture’s primary hand-calculation workflow uses r and the r table. The formula is stated for \(|r|<1\); a perfect nonconstant correlation is a boundary case.

13. Worked Example — Significance of the Lecture Correlation

For Lulu and the others, n = 4 and df = 2. With a nondirectional .05 test, \(r_{crit}=.950\). Our obtained \(r=.971008\) exceeds .950, so reject the null. The t transformation gives approximately 5.74 with 2 df; the two-sided p value is .0290.

A suitable report is: The two scores were strongly positively correlated, r(2) = .971, p = .029, n = 4. The very small sample calls for caution about precision and assumptions; the evidence does not demonstrate causation.

14. Applications: Prediction, Validity and Reliability

Lecture slide 28 identifies three central uses. For prediction, a relationship between study hours and grades can support an estimate of a grade from known study hours; correlation is the starting point, not an actual prediction equation. For validity and theory evaluation, a new attention task might correlate with an established attention measure as evidence of convergence; this alone cannot establish that the test measures only attention or verify a whole theory. For reliability, repeated administrations or two versions of a test can be correlated to assess stability of relative ordering. A high test–retest correlation does not guarantee agreement: if everyone scores ten points higher the second time, r may still equal 1. Further evidence of measurement agreement and design quality is needed.

15. What Can Go Wrong? Correlation and Causation

Slide 29 and Privitera (2018, Figure 13.11) distinguish the direction of a statistical association from the direction of causation. A negative relationship between mood and eating could be consistent with four causal stories: mood affects eating; eating affects mood; both influence each other; or a third factor influences both.

These diagrams are alternative hypotheses, not causal results. A coefficient alone cannot choose among them. A significant r does not solve the third-variable problem or the directionality problem. Rigorous controlled experiments with appropriate random assignment can offer stronger causal evidence. Longitudinal studies can establish temporal ordering and compare explanations, but without additional assumptions and design controls they cannot eliminate every plausible confound (lecture slide 42).

Slide 30 illustrates a spurious correlation using yearly cheese consumption and deaths from entanglement in bedsheets (reported r = +.947, attributed to Tyler Vigen). The figure actually plots two time series on separate vertical scales. The association is not evidence that cheese causes those deaths; shared time trends, chance selection among many series, and autocorrelation can all make such associations misleading. The ordinary independent-pairs inference would not automatically be justified for successive years.

16. Restriction of Range, Outliers, and Form

The lecture’s restriction-of-range illustration (slide 31) reports r = .07 for a narrow subset versus .85 for a wider sample. If a study contains only students with similar high entrance scores, the variation in X may be too limited to reveal a relationship across the full population. Range restriction often weakens r, but can also change the sign or increase the magnitude depending on selection and shape. Do not generalize beyond the observed range.

An outlier can weaken, strengthen or reverse a correlation. Slide 32 reports a change from .85 to .28 when a single high-X, low-Y observation is added. Cross-products give extreme deviations substantial influence. Check data entry and measurement context; analyze with and without the point where justified, and disclose the sensitivity. Do not delete a valid observation merely because it undermines a desired result.

The dinosaur scatterplot on slide 34 illustrates that a single summary can hide elaborate structure. For example, a symmetric U-shaped pattern may have r = 0 even though Y is determined by X. Neither a large nor a small coefficient substitutes for plotting the data.

17. Assumptions and Diagnostics (Textbook Support)

Privitera (2018, Section 13.5; 2024, Sections 15.3 and 15.6) emphasizes linearity, homoscedasticity and bivariate normality. For ordinary Pearson inference, also require independent paired observational units and a sampling design that supports the desired generalization.

Check Meaning How to investigate
Measurement and variability Quantitative variables with meaningful distances and nonzero variance Check units, coding, constant columns and missing values
Linearity A straight line describes the pattern reasonably well Scatterplot; residual plot for systematic curves
Homoscedasticity Conditional spread around the line is reasonably constant Look for a funnel-shaped scatter or residual plot
Bivariate normality Joint distribution is approximately normal for classical exact inference Marginal histograms/Q–Q plots and scatterplot; marginal normality alone is not sufficient
Independence Each row is an independent unit, though X and Y within it are paired Check recruitment, clustering, repeated trials and sampling design

Pearson r can be computed descriptively for nonnormal numerical data with nonzero variance; the assumptions govern interpretation and the validity of conventional inference. A failed diagnostic is not a fixed mathematical bias toward zero. Conversely, a nonsignificant Shapiro–Wilk test does not prove normality, particularly with small n. If the relationship is monotonic but curved or variables are ranked, consider Spearman; for other shapes, model the relationship appropriately rather than forcing a single coefficient.

18. Correlation as Effect Size and the Coefficient of Determination

Lecture slide 33 gives Cohen-style reference magnitudes: .10 small, .30 medium, .50 large. These are rough benchmarks, not universal boundaries or significance cutoffs. A negative r can be a large effect. Practical importance depends on the research context and uncertainty.

The coefficient of determination in simple least-squares regression with an intercept is \(R^2=r^2\). It is the proportion of sample variation in Y about its mean accounted for by the fitted linear relationship with X. The complementary proportion is \(1-r^2\).

\[ r=.971008\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^2=.942857\approx94.3\%. \]

Using the lecture’s rounded r = .97 gives .9409, about 94%. Both are reasonable rounded descriptions. The age–CARS example gives \(.229^2=.052441\) or approximately 5.2%. Squaring removes direction, so r = −.50 and +.50 both give 25%.

Do not read r = .70 as 70% explained variation: square first to get 49%. Nor does explained variation mean a percentage of people affected or a causal percentage. The lecture’s terminology of shared variation is a convenient description of linear association, not proof of mechanism.

Part IV — Other Correlation Coefficients

20. Spearman’s Rank-Order Correlation

Pearson measures the consistency of a linear relationship using the actual magnitudes of scores. Spearman measures the consistency of their ordering: a monotonic pattern can increase or decrease consistently without changing at a constant rate. Use it for ordinal data or an approximately monotonic nonlinear relationship (lecture slides 35–36; Privitera, 2024, Section 15.8). A U-shaped relationship is not monotonic and can defeat both statistics.

The course’s method is straightforward: rank X from smallest to largest, rank Y separately, keep ranks attached to the original rows, and compute Pearson’s r on these two rank columns. Use \(r_S\) for the sample coefficient and \(\rho_S\) for the population parameter. It is unitless and ranges from −1 to +1 for nonconstant ranks.

21. Worked Example — A Perfect Negative Rank Correlation

The lecture’s raw data (slides 37–39):

Name X Y Rank X Rank Y Rank X − 3 Rank Y − 3 Product
Lulu 3 12 1 5 −2 2 −4
Mya 4 6 2 4 −1 1 −1
Hogarth 5 5 3 3 0 0 0
Tiki 10 4 4 2 1 −1 −1
Mouchie 13 3 5 1 2 −2 −4
Total 15 15 0 0 −10

The raw scores show a curved decreasing relationship (Pearson r = −.7562). Rank means are 3, rank sums of squares are 10 each, and the rank sum of products is −10.

\[ r_S=\frac{-10}{\sqrt{10(10)}}=-1. \]

Every increase in X rank corresponds to a decrease in Y rank. This is perfect reverse ordering, not a perfect straight-line relationship in the raw units. The lecture gives \(r_S^2=1\); this concerns ranks, not 100% of the original Y variance explained by X.

22. Tied Ranks and the Textbook Shortcut

If two values occupy ranks 1 and 2, give both rank 1.5. If three values occupy ranks 4, 5 and 6, give all rank 5. The next value still occupies rank 7. Lecture slide 40 gives X = (3, 5, 6, 12, 3, 6, 6). In original row order the ranks are (1.5, 3, 5, 7, 1.5, 5, 5); in sorted order they are (1.5, 1.5, 3, 5, 5, 5, 7). The sum of ranks is \(n(n+1)/2=28\).

The textbook also introduces the shortcut

\[ r_S=1-\frac{6\sum D_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}, \]

where \(D_i\) is the difference between the two ranks for a pair. This identity is exact when there are no ties. With ties, use Pearson’s r on average ranks (the lecture’s method) rather than applying the untied formula unchanged.

The 2024 Example 15.2 (Table 15.5) illustrates why. The rank columns are (1.5, 1.5, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8) and (1, 3, 2, 6, 4, 7, 8, 5). Both means equal 4.5; rank sums of squares are 41.5 and 42, and SP = 32.

\[ r_S=\frac{32}{\sqrt{41.5(42)}}=.766481. \]

The shortcut yields .767857 from \(\sum D^2=19.5\). The difference is due to ties, not merely rounding as the textbook suggests. The software result of .766 matches the lecture’s exact rank calculation. The text says six mice, but its table contains eight paired observations; the calculation uses eight.

23. Testing Spearman’s Correlation

State \(H_0:\rho_S=0\) and \(H_1:\rho_S\ne0\) for a two-tailed test. Use Appendix C.6 of the 2018 textbook (or D.6 in 2024). Unlike Pearson’s table, select the row using n, not n−2. For n = 5 the two-tailed .05 critical magnitude is 1.000; for n = 8 it is .738; for n = 10 it is .648 (Privitera, 2018, Table 13.4). With untied ranks and independent observations, small-sample exact permutation inference is discrete: the lecture’s perfect reverse ordering at n = 5 has two-sided exact p = 2/120 = .0167. Do not interpret the blank cells in small-n tables as zero thresholds.

For tied ranks, the ordinary no-ties critical-value table is only an approximation to inference; software with an appropriate permutation test or a clearly stated approximation is preferable. The 2024 mouse example’s asymptotic p is about .027; the exact tie-aware permutation p can differ. Do not confuse the table row n with the df some reporting conventions place in parentheses.

24. Point-Biserial Correlation: One Binary Variable

A dichotomous variable has two categories. Code them consistently as 0 and 1, retain the continuous measurement in the second column, and compute ordinary Pearson’s r. This is called the point-biserial coefficient \(r_{pb}\) (slide 41; Privitera, 2018, Section 13.6). Examples include treatment assignment and symptom score, pet ownership and a quantitative well-being score. A positive sign means higher average Y for the category coded 1; it is not an intrinsic order among nominal categories. Switching codes reverses sign.

For an original teaching example, let X = (0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1), Y = (2, 4, 6, 4, 6, 8). The group means are 4 and 6, the grand mean is 5, \(SS_X=1.5\), \(SS_Y=22\), and \(SP=3\).

\[ r_{pb}=\frac{3}{\sqrt{1.5(22)}}=.5222. \]

The formula links directly to the pooled independent-samples t test of Week 7:

\[ r_{pb}^2=\frac{t^2}{t^2+df},\qquad |t|=\sqrt{\frac{r_{pb}^2df}{1-r_{pb}^2}},\qquad df=n-2. \]

The sign of t depends on the contrast order; for mean of group 1 (code 1) minus mean of group 0 it agrees with r. In the example, pooled variance is 4, SE = \(\sqrt{4/3+4/3}=1.633\), and t(4) = 1.225, p = .288. The same decision follows from r. The equivalence is for the pooled test, not generally Welch’s test. Check independent observations, within-group normality for classical inference, and similar variances.

The textbook’s alternative uses group proportions p and q and the SD with divisor n: \(r_{pb}=(M_1-M_0)\sqrt{pq}/\sqrt{SS_Y/n}\). Use the stated divisor; substituting an n−1 SD without adjustment gives a different result. For the 2024 laughter example (Table 15.8), code male-attributed voice 0 and female-attributed voice 1. Means 17 and 20, n = 12, \(SS_Y=984.25\) from unrounded data, and \(r_{pb}=+.16331\), p = .612. The book’s hand calculation uses the reverse mean contrast, giving a negative sign; the coding convention resolves the discrepancy. Neither contrast is significant.

25. Phi: Two Binary Variables

Phi (\(\phi\)) is Pearson correlation applied to two binary columns (both 0/1). It can also be computed from a 2 × 2 count table, as in Privitera (2018, Table 13.5; 2024, Table 15.13). Here is the 2024 hypothetical example with consistent codes:

Employment 0 Employment 1 Total
Happiness 0 14 (a) 6 (b) 20
Happiness 1 6 (c) 14 (d) 20
Total 20 20 40

\[ \phi=\frac{ad-bc}{\sqrt{(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)}}=\frac{196-36}{400}=+.40. \]

For uncorrected Pearson chi-square in a 2 × 2 table,

\[ \chi^2=N\phi^2=40(.16)=6.40,\qquad |\phi|=\sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{N}}. \]

At df = 1 and alpha .05, chi-square critical = 3.84, p = .0114, so reject independence. The expected frequency in each cell is 10 (row total × column total / N), satisfying the introductory expected-count guideline of at least 5. The square-root formula gives magnitude only; recover direction from the coding or cross-product difference. This relation uses uncorrected chi-square, not Yates-corrected or Fisher’s exact test. A small or sparse table may require exact inference. Each participant should contribute to one cell only. Switching one binary coding reverses phi’s sign, not strength. Week 11 will revisit categorical-data tests.

29. Formula Map and Common Mistakes

Task Formula or decision
Sample means \(\bar{x}=\sum X/n\), \(\bar{y}=\sum Y/n\)
Sum of squares \(SS_X=\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2\); analogous for Y
Sum of products \(SP=\sum(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})\)
Sample covariance \(SP/(n-1)\)
Pearson \(SP/\sqrt{SS_XSS_Y}\) or \(\operatorname{Cov}_{X,Y}/(s_Xs_Y)\)
Pearson hypothesis test \(H_0:\rho=0\), df = n−2; C.5
Determination \(r^2\); multiply by 100 for percent
Spearman Pearson on average ranks; C.6 uses n
Point-biserial Pearson on binary code and Y; \(r_{pb}^2=t^2/(t^2+df)\)
Phi Pearson on binary codes; \(\chi^2=N\phi^2\)

Typical errors: losing the negative signs on cross-products; sorting independently; treating SP as covariance or using n instead of n−1; omitting the square root in the denominator; comparing signed r with only a positive critical value; using n−1 for Pearson df; calling r = .40 40% explained variance; assigning arbitrary distinct ranks to ties; using Pearson’s table for Spearman; interpreting a binary code as a natural numerical order; and drawing a causal conclusion from a significant r.

Question 1 — Choosing a research design

For each student, a researcher records weekly study hours and exam grade. She wants to describe their linear association. Which procedure is appropriate?

  • A. Pearson correlation
  • B. Paired t test
  • C. One-sample t test
  • D. One-way ANOVA
TipAnswer

Correct answer: A. Pearson correlation

NoteExplanation

Both variables are numerical and each row represents the same student. The research question concerns whether high study-hour values tend to accompany high grades, not whether two means differ. A paired t test would require a meaningful difference between measurements on a comparable scale; subtracting hours from grades has no useful interpretation.

Question 2 — Strength and direction

Which coefficient has the strongest linear association?

  • A. +.42
  • B. −.81
  • C. +.76
  • D. −.05
TipAnswer

Correct answer: B. −.81

NoteExplanation

Compare absolute values, not signed numerical order. The magnitude .81 is greater than .76, .42 and .05. Its negative sign indicates that larger values on one variable tend to accompany smaller values on the other; it says nothing about causal direction.

Question 3 — Reading a scatterplot

A scatterplot has a narrow downward-sloping cloud of points. Which coefficient is most plausible?

  • A. +.85
  • B. +.05
  • C. −.85
  • D. 0.00
TipAnswer

Correct answer: C. −.85

NoteExplanation

The negative slope indicates opposite movement of the variables. The narrow cloud suggests that their linear relationship is consistent. A positive coefficient would contradict the slope, while a near-zero coefficient would generally be inconsistent with the described tight linear pattern.

Question 4 — Cross-product signs

A participant has X = 8 and Y = 2; the sample means are 5 and 4. What is this pair’s contribution to SP?

  • A. +6
  • B. −2
  • C. +10
  • D. −6
TipAnswer

Correct answer: D. −6

\((8-5)(2-4)=3(-2)=-6\).

NoteExplanation

The X score is above its mean but the Y score is below its mean. Multiplying the signed deviations produces a negative contribution. Do not square the deviations as you would for variance: the opposite signs carry information about the association.

Question 5 — Covariance

For six complete pairs, SP = 30. What is the sample covariance?

  • A. 6
  • B. 5
  • C. 30
  • D. 36
TipAnswer

Correct answer: A. 6

\(30/(6-1)=6\).

NoteExplanation

Covariance is the sum of cross-products adjusted by the sample degrees of freedom. Six observations give five degrees of freedom for this calculation. Dividing by six would use a different convention; dividing by four would confuse the covariance calculation with the significance test of Pearson r.

Question 6 — Pearson from sums of squares

Suppose SP = −12, SSX = 16 and SSY = 25. What is r?

  • A. −.03
  • B. −.60
  • C. +.60
  • D. −.29
TipAnswer

Correct answer: B. −.60

\(-12/\sqrt{16(25)}=-12/20=-.60\).

NoteExplanation

The numerator gives the sign of the association; the denominator is a positive scale factor. The square root of the product of the two sums of squares is 20, not 400 or 41. A value of −.60 describes a relatively large negative linear association under the lecture’s guidelines.

Question 7 — Measurement units

Sleep is recorded in hours and fatigue in points. Sleep hours are converted to minutes, with fatigue unchanged. What happens?

  • A. Both covariance and r stay the same
  • B. Both multiply by 60
  • C. Covariance multiplies by 60; r is unchanged
  • D. r changes sign
TipAnswer

Correct answer: C. Covariance multiplies by 60; r is unchanged

NoteExplanation

The conversion multiplies each sleep deviation by 60. It therefore multiplies the covariance and the sleep SD by the same factor. These changes cancel in Pearson’s formula, so the observed strength and direction stay the same even though the covariance now has different units.

Question 8 — Population hypotheses

Which is the correct nondirectional null and alternative for testing Pearson correlation?

  • A. \(H_0:r=0\); \(H_1:r\ne0\)
  • B. \(H_0:\rho=1\); \(H_1:\rho\ne1\)
  • C. \(H_0:\rho=0\); \(H_1:\rho>0\)
  • D. \(H_0:\rho=0\); \(H_1:\rho\ne0\)
TipAnswer

Correct answer: D. \(H_0:\rho=0\); \(H_1:\rho\ne0\)

NoteExplanation

Hypotheses are statements about population parameters, here rho. A nondirectional alternative allows both positive and negative departures from zero. Sample r is the calculated evidence, not the unknown population parameter in the hypotheses.

Question 9 — Degrees of freedom

A study has 18 independent participants, each with a pair of scores. What Pearson table row should be used?

  • A. 16
  • B. 17
  • C. 18
  • D. 36
TipAnswer

Correct answer: A. 16

\(df=18-2=16\).

NoteExplanation

The sample size is the number of pairs, not the total number of recorded values. Pearson’s critical-value table uses n minus two. This is different from the paired t test, which uses n minus one, and Spearman’s course table, which uses n itself.

Question 10 — Significance and sign

For n = 12, a two-tailed .05 Pearson test has \(r_{crit}=.576\). The observed r is −.62. What is the decision?

  • A. Fail to reject because −.62 is below +.576
  • B. Reject because .62 exceeds .576
  • C. Reject only if r is positive
  • D. Fail to reject because df = 12
TipAnswer

Correct answer: B. Reject because .62 exceeds .576

NoteExplanation

A two-tailed test has both a positive and a negative rejection boundary. The obtained coefficient is more negative than −.576, or equivalently its magnitude exceeds .576. The result supports a nonzero negative population linear association, conditional on the assumptions.

Question 11 — Coefficient of determination

A correlation of −.40 is observed. What proportion of Y variation is accounted for by the simple fitted linear model?

  • A. −40%
  • B. 40%
  • C. 16%
  • D. 84%
TipAnswer

Correct answer: C. 16%

\((-.40)^2=.16\).

NoteExplanation

Square the coefficient before converting it to a percentage. The negative sign describes direction but disappears when the coefficient is squared. The remaining 84% is not accounted for by that fitted linear model; the 16% does not imply that X causes changes in Y.

Question 12 — Nonlinearity

X takes the values −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 and Y equals X squared. Which is correct?

  • A. Pearson r is +1
  • B. Spearman r is +1
  • C. There is no relationship of any kind
  • D. Pearson r is zero despite a deterministic relationship
TipAnswer

Correct answer: D. Pearson r is zero despite a deterministic relationship

NoteExplanation

As X moves from −2 to 0, Y decreases; from 0 to 2, it increases. The symmetric cross-products cancel, yielding zero Pearson correlation, although Y is determined by X. Spearman does not solve this U-shaped problem because the relationship is not monotonic.

Question 13 — Rank correlation

Two judges rank the same ten performances from best to worst. Which coefficient is most appropriate for comparing their ordering?

  • A. Spearman
  • B. Phi
  • C. Point-biserial
  • D. Pooled t
TipAnswer

Correct answer: A. Spearman

NoteExplanation

The data are ordinal and the relevant question is consistency of rank order. Spearman uses Pearson’s computation after converting scores to ranks, or directly on valid ranks. Both judges must use a consistent ranking direction and tied ranks must be averaged.

Question 14 — Average ranks

The sorted scores are 2, 4, 4, 4, 9. Which ranks should be assigned?

  • A. 1, 2, 2, 2, 3
  • B. 1, 3, 3, 3, 5
  • C. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5
  • D. 1, 4, 4, 4, 5

Correct answer: B. 1, 3, 3, 3, 5

NoteExplanation

The three observations equal to 4 occupy positions 2, 3 and 4. Their average position is 3, and each receives that rank. The final observation still occupies position 5; using dense ranks would not follow the course’s average-rank method.

Question 15 — Binary and continuous data

A researcher records tutoring status (0 = no, 1 = yes) and numerical exam grades. Which coefficient specifically describes this association?

  • A. Phi
  • B. Spearman only
  • C. Point-biserial
  • D. One-sample t
TipAnswer

Correct answer: C. Point-biserial

NoteExplanation

Point-biserial is Pearson correlation with one dichotomous and one quantitative variable. A positive coefficient under this coding means the tutored group has a higher mean grade. It is mathematically linked to the pooled two-group t test, but the coefficient alone cannot establish the causal benefit of tutoring.

Question 16 — Two binary variables

In an independent 2 × 2 table with N = 100, uncorrected chi-square = 9. What is the magnitude of phi?

  • A. .09
  • B. 9.00
  • C. .90
  • D. .30
TipAnswer

Correct answer: D. .30

\(|\phi|=\sqrt{9/100}=.30\).

NoteExplanation

The chi-square relationship gives squared phi when divided by N, so take the square root. The sign is not recoverable from chi-square because squaring removes it. The original binary coding and cross-product difference are needed to determine whether the signed coefficient is positive or negative.

Question 17 — Identify the statistical object

For each study, select the method and identify the observational unit: (a) 15 people complete the same anxiety scale before and after an intervention; (b) 15 people provide hours of sleep and attention scores; (c) 30 unrelated people are allocated to three conditions and tested once.

TipAnswer

(a) Paired t test, one difference per person. (b) Pearson correlation if the relationship is linear, one pair per person. (c) One-way between-subjects ANOVA, one outcome per person.

NoteExplanation

The designs differ in the structure of the question. The first concerns average within-person change on a common scale. The second concerns co-variation across people, while the third concerns differences between independent group means. Identify the outcome or paired variables and the research question before selecting a formula.

Question 19 — A complete Pearson significance test

A sample of five independent pairs gives r = −.80. At two-tailed .05, the Pearson critical value for df = 3 is .878. State the hypotheses, decision, and interpretation.

TipAnswer

\(H_0:\rho=0\), \(H_1:\rho\ne0\); \(df=5-2=3\). Since \(|r|=.80<.878\), fail to reject. The exact parametric p value is .1041.

NoteExplanation

Although the observed coefficient is large, a sample of only five pairs has considerable sampling uncertainty. The negative coefficient has to cross −.878 to be significant at this threshold. A failure to reject is not proof that rho is zero; more data may help distinguish a real negative association from sampling variability.

Question 20 — Calculate by two equivalent routes

X = (2, 4, 6, 8), Y = (1, 3, 2, 6). Use both covariance and SP formulas to obtain r.

TipAnswer

Means: 5 and 3. Deviations: X (−3, −1, 1, 3), Y (−2, 0, −1, 3).

\(SP=6+0-1+9=14\), \(SS_X=20\), \(SS_Y=14\).

\[ \operatorname{Cov}=14/3=4.6667,\quad s_X=\sqrt{20/3},\quad s_Y=\sqrt{14/3}. \]

\[ r=\frac{14/3}{\sqrt{20/3}\sqrt{14/3}}=\frac{14}{\sqrt{20(14)}}=.83666. \]

NoteExplanation

Both calculations are the same identity written in different forms. The common divisor n−1 cancels when covariance is standardized by both sample SDs. The positive cross-products at the smallest and largest X values dominate the one negative cross-product. Keep the square roots and full intermediate precision to avoid rounding discrepancies.

Question 21 — Changing units and coding

A dataset has covariance = −12, X SD = 3, Y SD = 5. Compute r. Then replace X by 10 + 2X, and separately replace Y by −Y in the original data. Give the new covariance and r in each case.

TipAnswer

Original: \(r=-12/(3\times5)=-.80\). For \(X^*=10+2X\), covariance = −24, X SD = 6, r = −.80. For \(Y^*=-Y\), covariance = +12, Y SD = 5, r = +.80.

NoteExplanation

The addition of 10 changes location but not deviations. Multiplying X by two doubles both the covariance and its corresponding SD, so the standardized association stays the same. Negating Y reverses its signed deviations while leaving its SD unchanged; therefore only the sign of r reverses.

Question 22 — Sample size and a fixed effect

Two studies both report r = .60. One has n = 10 and the other n = 25. At two-tailed .05, the respective critical magnitudes are .632 and .396. Make both decisions and explain.

TipAnswer

n = 10: fail to reject (.60 < .632; p ≈ .0667). n = 25: reject (.60 > .396; p ≈ .0015). The descriptive effect size is identical in both samples.

NoteExplanation

A larger sample makes a correlation of this magnitude less likely under the zero-correlation null model. The threshold drops as n increases, even though the definition of r is unchanged. It is inappropriate to conclude that the second association is larger solely because it is significant; both sample coefficients are .60.

Question 23 — Effect size and reporting

A study of 30 participants gives r = .35. The two-tailed .05 critical magnitude is .361. Calculate the coefficient of determination and write a one-sentence result.

TipAnswer

\(r^2=.1225=12.25\%\). Fail to reject; r(28) = .35, p = .058 (approximately), n = 30. The sample shows a positive linear association, but the test does not reach .05.

NoteExplanation

The magnitude is around the lecture’s medium-effect reference point, regardless of significance. The fitted straight line accounts for about 12% of observed Y variation, but the data do not provide enough evidence at the specified threshold to reject a zero population correlation. Reporting both results avoids equating statistical significance with effect size.

Question 24 — Causal alternatives

A significant positive correlation is observed between hours of social-media use and loneliness. Describe four causal explanations consistent with this observation and one design that could improve causal evidence.

TipAnswer

Social-media use could increase loneliness; loneliness could increase social-media use; both could reinforce one another; or a third factor such as limited opportunities for social contact could influence both. A carefully designed randomized intervention varying social-media exposure, with ethical safeguards and a suitable comparison condition, could improve evidence about the causal effect.

NoteExplanation

The correlation is symmetric, so it cannot distinguish the order of influence. Even observing a temporal sequence does not eliminate all confounders. A controlled experiment addresses some competing explanations, while a longitudinal observational design can clarify ordering without automatically establishing causation.

Question 25 — Range restriction and an influential point

A researcher reports r = .12 between aptitude and grades in a sample restricted to high aptitude. In a wider sample r = .70, but adding one extreme discordant observation lowers it to .28. Explain both changes and how to investigate them.

TipAnswer

The restricted sample may have too little aptitude variability to reveal the broader pattern. The extreme point can strongly influence SP and the denominator. Plot all observations, check sampling and data integrity, investigate the observation’s context, and report justified sensitivity analyses rather than automatically deleting it.

NoteExplanation

Correlation describes the observations actually sampled. Limiting the X range often reduces signal relative to scatter, but the direction of the change is not mathematically guaranteed. An extreme observation can dominate a cross-product and alter both means and spreads. The appropriate response is to examine the data and justify any exclusion independently of the desired result.

Question 26 — Calculate Spearman without ties

For X = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5) and Y = (5, 3, 4, 1, 2), calculate Spearman using ranks and the shortcut. At n = 5, the two-tailed .05 Spearman critical value is 1.000.

TipAnswer

The values already are ranks. Means = 3 each, \(SP=-8\), \(SS_X=SS_Y=10\), so \(r_S=-.80\). Rank differences are (−4, −1, −1, 3, 3), with squared sum 36.

\[ r_S=1-\frac{6(36)}{5(25-1)}=-.80. \]

Fail to reject at .05 because .80 < 1.000.

NoteExplanation

Both methods give the same answer because the ranks are untied. Unlike Pearson’s significance table, the Spearman table uses five observations rather than three degrees of freedom. The ordering is strongly negative but imperfect. Exact inference for such a small sample is discrete, requiring perfect ordering to reach the course’s two-tailed .05 threshold.

Question 27 — Compute Spearman with ties

Five paired observations have X = (1, 1, 3, 4, 5), Y = (5, 3, 4, 2, 1). Compute average ranks and Pearson correlation of the ranks.

TipAnswer

Question 28 — Point-biserial and the pooled t test

Four people in a comparison group (code 0) have scores (2, 4, 4, 6); four in a training group (code 1) have scores (4, 6, 6, 8). Calculate the group means, pooled variance, t, point-biserial r and its squared value. Use two-tailed .05 critical t = 2.447.

TipAnswer

Means are 4 and 6. Each group has SS = 8, so \(s_p^2=(8+8)/6=8/3\).

\[ SE=\sqrt{(8/3)/4+(8/3)/4}=\sqrt{4/3}=1.1547,\qquad t(6)=2/1.1547=1.7321. \]

\[ r_{pb}^2=3/(3+6)=1/3,\qquad r_{pb}=+.57735. \]

Fail to reject; p ≈ .1340.

NoteExplanation

The two-group test assesses the mean difference relative to its pooled standard error. Point-biserial standardizes the relationship between group coding and the numerical outcome. Both give the same significance decision when the ordinary pooled t assumptions apply. The positive sign follows because the group coded 1 has the higher mean; reverse the codes and it reverses.

Question 29 — Phi from counts

A survey records two binary variables. Rows are X = 0, 1 and columns Y = 0, 1. The observed counts are (18, 12) in row 0 and (6, 24) in row 1. Compute phi, uncorrected chi-square, and decision at .05 (critical chi-square = 3.84).

TipAnswer

Row totals 30 and 30; column totals 24 and 36; N = 60.

\[ \phi=\frac{18(24)-12(6)}{\sqrt{30(30)(24)(36)}}=.408248. \]

\[ \chi^2=60(.408248^2)=10.00. \]

Expected counts are 12 and 18 in each row. With df = 1, reject independence (p ≈ .00157).

NoteExplanation

The cross-product difference is positive because concordant pairs contribute more strongly than discordant pairs. The denominator adjusts for the margins, so the result is a standardized association, not a raw count difference. The expected counts satisfy the introductory chi-square guideline. The result does not establish that either categorical characteristic causes the other.

Question 30 — Reliability, agreement and regression to the mean

A test is administered twice, and every student’s second score is exactly 5 points higher. What is the test–retest correlation if the first scores vary? Does this establish agreement? Separately, explain regression toward the mean for students selected because their first scores are unusually low.

TipAnswer

The correlation is +1 because the second score is the first plus a constant. Absolute agreement is not perfect: all second scores are five points higher. With imperfect test–retest association, a group selected for extreme low scores may on average score closer to the mean on a retest even without an intervention.

NoteExplanation

Pearson correlation measures preservation of a linear pattern, not equality of values. Adding a constant leaves all deviations and the coefficient unchanged, so a systematic measurement shift can coexist with perfect rank consistency. Regression toward the mean arises from imperfect association and selection on extremes, not necessarily from true improvement.

Week 9 Takeaway

Correlation measures how consistently two variables vary together. Pearson’s r standardizes covariance; Spearman applies the same idea to ranks; point-biserial and phi use binary codes. A coefficient alone does not show the shape of the data, prove a relationship in the population, or explain why it exists.

Plot first, preserve the pairs, choose the coefficient that fits the question, calculate carefully, and separate statistical evidence from causal claims.
