Overview

This page is a single integrated review map for the PSYC 210 midterm. It is designed to help you see how the first six weeks fit together as one sequence of statistical reasoning.

It does not replace the six weekly Study Guides. Instead, it answers a different question:

What do I need to connect across Weeks 1–6 in order to prepare efficiently for the midterm?

The official Midterm Core Concept Guide is itself a focused review rather than a complete list of all testable material. Students are still responsible for the relevant lecture and reading material.

The exam includes both conceptual questions and computational questions, so this page is organized around both kinds of thinking.

Part I — The Midterm as One Connected Story

1. The Six-Week Logic

The first six weeks can be understood as one progression:

Week 1 — How scientific questions become data

↓

Week 2 — How variables are measured, organized, and displayed

↓

Week 3 — How distributions are summarized

↓

Week 4 — How one score is located relative to a population

↓

Week 5 — How sample means vary from sample to sample

↓

Week 6 — How sample evidence is used to test a population claim

That progression matters because later topics depend directly on earlier ones.

For example:

  • you cannot interpret a z score without understanding the mean and standard deviation;
  • you cannot understand standard error without understanding variability;
  • you cannot understand hypothesis testing without understanding the sampling distribution;
  • you cannot interpret statistical significance responsibly without understanding effect size and confidence intervals.

2. The Three Questions Behind the Midterm

Almost every problem on the midterm can be reduced to one of three questions:

Question A — What kind of data or design is this?

This draws mainly from Weeks 1–2.

Examples:

  • experimental, quasi-experimental, or correlational?
  • nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio?
  • categorical or continuous?
  • bar chart or histogram?

Question B — What does this distribution look like?

This draws mainly from Weeks 2–4.

Examples:

  • what is the mean, median, mode, variance, or SD?
  • is the distribution skewed?
  • how far is a score from the mean?
  • what probability corresponds to a z score?

Question C — What can we infer from this sample?

This draws mainly from Weeks 5–6.

Examples:

  • what is the standard error?
  • how unusual is a sample mean?
  • should we reject \(H_0\)?
  • what is the p value?
  • what is the effect size?
  • what does the confidence interval tell us?
  • what affects power?

Part II — Week-by-Week Exam Map

3. Week 1 — Scientific Reasoning and Statistical Language

Know conceptually

  • goals of science;
  • theories, variables, and hypotheses;
  • experimental vs. quasi-experimental vs. correlational designs;
  • descriptive vs. inferential statistics;
  • populations vs. samples.

Be able to compute

  • order of operations;
  • indexed-variable notation;
  • summation notation.

High-yield distinction

Descriptive statistics stay with the observed data. Inferential statistics generalize from a sample to a population.

Common trap

\[ \left(\sum x\right)^2 \]

is not the same as:

\[ \sum x^2. \]

4. Week 2 — Measurement, Frequency, and Visualization

Know conceptually

  • categorical vs. continuous variables;
  • NOIR scales;
  • frequency distributions;
  • grouped frequency distributions;
  • appropriate graph selection.

Be able to compute

  • frequency;
  • proportion;
  • percentage;
  • cumulative frequency;
  • cumulative percentage;
  • bin width.

High-yield distinction

Scale Main information
Nominal category
Ordinal category + order
Interval equal units, arbitrary zero
Ratio equal units, meaningful zero

Common trap

Bar charts are for categorical variables and bars do not touch. Histograms are for continuous variables and bars touch.

5. Week 3 — Center, Spread, and Shape

Know conceptually

  • mode, median, mean;
  • range, IQR, variance, SD;
  • skewness;
  • kurtosis conceptually;
  • sample statistic vs. population parameter.

Be able to compute

  • mean;
  • weighted mean;
  • interpolated median/percentiles;
  • variance;
  • SD.

High-yield relationship

A positively skewed distribution usually has:

\[ \text{mean}>\text{median}. \]

A negatively skewed distribution usually has:

\[ \text{mean}<\text{median}. \]

Common trap

For this course, grouped/tied percentile problems use the instructor’s interpolation formula, not the simple “middle observation” shortcut.

6. Week 4 — z Scores and the Normal Distribution

Know conceptually

  • properties of the normal distribution;
  • probability as area;
  • meaning of a z score;
  • use of the z table.

Be able to compute

\[ Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}. \]

High-yield interpretation

A z score tells you:

how many population standard deviations a score lies above or below the mean.

Common trap

A negative z score means “below the mean,” not “bad,” and probabilities themselves are never negative.

7. Week 5 — Sampling Distributions and the CLT

Know conceptually

  • basic probability;
  • AND, OR, and conditional probability;
  • independent vs. dependent events;
  • Law of Large Numbers;
  • Gambler’s Fallacy;
  • sampling error;
  • sampling distributions;
  • CLT.

Be able to compute

\[ \sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \]

and:

\[ Z = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\sigma_{\bar{x}}}. \]

High-yield relationship

As:

\[ n\uparrow, \]

then:

\[ SE\downarrow. \]

So the same raw mean difference becomes more unusual when the sample is larger.

Common trap

For an individual score, divide by \(\sigma\). For a sample mean, divide by the standard error.

8. Week 6 — Hypothesis Testing and Interpretation

Know conceptually

  • NHST;
  • \(H_0\) and \(H_1\);
  • alpha;
  • critical values;
  • Type I and Type II errors;
  • statistical power;
  • effect size;
  • confidence intervals.

Be able to compute

\[ Z_{obt} = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu} {\sigma/\sqrt n} \]

\[ ES = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\sigma} \]

\[ CI = \bar{x} \pm Z_{crit}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right). \]

High-yield decision language

Use:

  • reject \(H_0\);
  • fail to reject \(H_0\).

Do not say that the null hypothesis has been proven true.

Common trap

Statistical significance and effect size answer different questions.

Part III — Formula Map

9. Which Formula Belongs to Which Task?

Task Formula Week
Bin width \(\frac{\max-\min}{\#\text{ bins}}\) 2
Mean \(\bar{x}=\frac{\sum x_i}{n}\) 3
Combined mean \(\bar{x}_C=\frac{\sum n_i\bar{x}_i}{\sum n_i}\) 3
Range \(\max-\min\) 3
IQR \(P_{75}-P_{25}\) 3
Sample SD \(s=\sqrt{\frac{\sum(x-\bar{x})^2}{n-1}}\) 3
Raw-score z \(Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\) 4
Basic probability \(P(A)=\frac{\#A}{\#S}\) 5
Independent AND \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\) 5
Mutually exclusive OR \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)\) 5
Standard error \(\sigma_{\bar{x}}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\) 5
Sample-mean z \(Z=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\sigma_{\bar{x}}}\) 5–6
Effect size \(ES=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\sigma}\) 6
Confidence interval \(CI=\bar{x}\pm Z_{crit}\sigma_{\bar{x}}\) 6

The most important exam skill is not memorizing isolated equations. It is recognizing which quantity the denominator should represent.

10. Three Denominators That Students Commonly Mix Up

Raw-score z

\[ Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma} \]

Denominator: population SD.

Sample-mean z

\[ Z=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\sigma_{\bar{x}}} \]

Denominator: standard error.

Effect size

\[ ES=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\sigma} \]

Denominator: population SD, not standard error.

This distinction is extremely high yield.

Part IV — Cross-Week Connections

11. Mean, SD, z, and Probability

Week 3 gives you the mean and SD.

Week 4 turns those into a standardized distance:

\[ Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}. \]

Then the z table turns that standardized distance into probability.

So:

center + spread → z → probability

12. SD vs. Standard Error

Standard deviation describes spread among individual scores.

Standard error describes spread among sample means.

\[ SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

As \(n\) increases, sample means cluster more tightly around the population mean.

So:

SD describes people/scores; SE describes sample means.

13. Sampling Distribution to Hypothesis Testing

Week 5 asks:

What sample means would we expect if the population mean were \(\mu\)?

Week 6 asks:

Is our observed sample mean too unusual under that expectation?

That is why the hypothesis-test statistic is:

\[ Z_{obt} = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu} {SE}. \]

14. Significance, Effect Size, and Confidence Intervals

These should be read together.

Significance

Is the observed result sufficiently unusual under \(H_0\)?

Effect size

How large is the standardized mean difference?

Confidence interval

What range of population values is compatible with the estimate under the course procedure?

A statistically significant result can still have a small effect size.

15. Sample Size Connects Weeks 5 and 6

Because:

\[ SE=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}, \]

larger samples reduce SE.

This makes the same raw mean difference produce a larger absolute z statistic.

Therefore:

\[ n\uparrow \Rightarrow SE\downarrow \Rightarrow |Z|\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{power}\uparrow. \]

This is one of the most important cross-week relationships on the midterm.

Part V — Fast Decision Rules

16. If You See This, Think This

If the question mentions… Think…
categories or ranks measurement scale
“or less” cumulative frequency
middle / typical score central tendency
spread / variability range, IQR, variance, SD
long tail skewness
score relative to population raw-score z
all possible sample means sampling distribution
variability of sample means standard error
sample mean vs. known \(\mu\) sample-mean z
“unlikely if \(H_0\) is true” NHST / p value
reject a true \(H_0\) Type I error
fail to reject a false \(H_0\) Type II error
detect a real effect power
magnitude of effect effect size
plausible range around estimate confidence interval

17. Midterm Mistakes to Avoid

  • averaging group means when group sizes differ;
  • using \(n\) instead of \(n-1\) for sample variance;
  • confusing positive skew with “more high scores” rather than a tail toward high values;
  • using \(\sigma\) instead of SE for a sample-mean z;
  • interpreting a p value as the probability that \(H_0\) is true;
  • saying “accept \(H_0\)” instead of “fail to reject \(H_0\)”;
  • interpreting statistical significance as effect size;
  • thinking a larger sample changes the raw effect size itself;
  • forgetting to double a one-tail z-table area for a two-tailed p value.

Block A — Scientific Reasoning, Variables, and Measurement

Problem 1 — Experimental Design

Multiple Choice

A researcher randomly assigns participants to caffeine or placebo and measures reaction time. What design is used?

  • A. Experimental
  • B. Quasi-experimental
  • C. Correlational
  • D. Case study
TipAnswer

Correct answer: A. Experimental

NoteExplanation

Random assignment plus manipulation makes this an experiment.

This question focuses on Experimental Design. The key is to identify how the study is structured before thinking about any numbers. Ask whether a condition was manipulated, whether participants were randomly assigned, and what outcome was measured. Manipulation plus random assignment supports an experimental design; measurement without manipulation is correlational, and lack of random assignment usually points toward a quasi-experimental design. A common mistake is to infer causation merely because two variables differ or are associated.

Problem 2 — Correlation and Causation

Multiple Choice

A study measures extroversion and GPA without manipulation. What conclusion is supported?

  • A. Association but not causation
  • B. A causal effect
  • C. Random assignment
  • D. No relationship can be studied
TipAnswer

Correct answer: A. Association but not causation

NoteExplanation

Correlational data can establish association, not causation by itself.

This question focuses on Correlation and Causation. This question asks you to identify the statistical object involved and then apply the rule that belongs to it. A reliable sequence is: write down what is given, name what is being asked for, choose the matching definition or formula, and only then calculate. After obtaining the result, interpret it in the language of the problem rather than stopping at a number. That identify–choose–calculate–interpret sequence is useful throughout Weeks 1–6.

Problem 3 — Population and Sample

Multiple Choice

A study samples 300 SFU students to represent all SFU undergraduates. What is the population?

  • A. All SFU undergraduates
  • B. The 300 sampled students
  • C. Only complete responders
  • D. All Canadian university students
TipAnswer

Correct answer: A. All SFU undergraduates

NoteExplanation

The population is the entire target group.

This question focuses on Population and Sample. The important distinction is the level at which the statement applies. A statistic summarizes the observed sample, whereas a parameter describes the population; descriptive statements stay with the observed data, while inferential statements generalize beyond them. Identify first who was actually measured and then the larger group the researcher wants to understand. This prevents the common error of reversing sample/population or statistic/parameter.

Problem 8 — Summation

Multiple Choice

For X={4,7,3,8,6}, what is sum X?

  • A. 28
  • B. 174
  • C. 784
  • D. 5.6
TipAnswer

Correct answer: A. 28

NoteExplanation

This question focuses on Summation. This question tests careful reading of mathematical notation. Indices determine which observations are included, while parentheses and exponents determine the order of operations; for example, ΣX² is not the same as (ΣX)². Block C — Central Tendency, Variability, and Shape

Problem 17 — Center Measures

Multiple Choice

For X={2,3,3,4,8}, which set is correct?

  • A. Mode=3, Median=3, Mean=4
  • B. Mode=4, Median=3, Mean=3
  • C. Mode=3, Median=4, Mean=3
  • D. Mode=8, Median=3, Mean=4
TipAnswer

Correct answer: A. Mode=3, Median=3, Mean=4

NoteExplanation

The mode and median are 3; the mean is 4.

This question focuses on Center Measures. The question asks which feature of the distribution is being summarized. The mode depends on frequency, the median on rank position, and the mean on the magnitude of every score, so extreme values affect them differently. Skewness is described by the direction of the long tail, which usually pulls the mean toward it. Choose the statistic that matches both the distribution’s shape and the type of summary requested.

Problem 18 — Outlier Resistance

Multiple Choice

Which center is more resistant to one extreme high outlier?

  • A. Median
  • B. Mean
  • C. Variance
  • D. Range
TipAnswer

Correct answer: A. Median

NoteExplanation

The median is based on rank.

This question focuses on Outlier Resistance. This item measures how far scores tend to lie from their mean. Because unsquared deviations cancel to zero, the deviations are squared before being summed; for a sample, divide by n−1 to obtain the sample variance. Taking the square root returns the measure of spread to the original units as the standard deviation. A good check is that variance cannot be negative and SD must equal the square root of the variance.

Problem 19 — Combined Mean

Multiple Choice

Group A: n=10, mean=8. Group B: n=30, mean=14. Combined mean?

  • A. 12.5
  • B. 11.0
  • C. 14.0
  • D. 22.0
TipAnswer

Correct answer: A. 12.5

NoteExplanation

[10(8)+30(14)]/40=12.5.

This question focuses on Combined Mean. A combined mean must reflect how many observations each group contributes. When sample sizes differ, simply averaging the group means gives the smaller group too much influence. The correct method weights each mean by its group size, adds those weighted totals, and divides by the combined sample size. The final answer should lie between the group means and be pulled toward the larger group.

Problem 43 — Critical Values and Decision Rule

For a two-tailed z test at \(\alpha=.05\), state critical values and decision rule.

TipAnswer

\(z_{crit}=\pm1.96\); reject when \(|Z_{obt}|\ge1.96\), otherwise fail to reject.

NoteExplanation

Midterm Takeaway

The midterm is not six disconnected topics.

It is one chain:

research design → measurement → description → standardization → sampling → inference

If you understand that chain, the formulas become easier to choose because each formula answers a different question.

The most important habit is therefore:

Identify what statistical object you are working with before calculating anything.

Is it:

  • a raw score?
  • a sample mean?
  • a distribution?
  • a probability?
  • a population claim?

Once that is clear, the correct method usually follows.
