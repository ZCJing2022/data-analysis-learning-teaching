Week 02 — Data Types, Frequency Distributions, and Visualization

Overview

Week 2 develops the first major set of descriptive-statistical tools for organizing psychological data. The central question is simple:

Once data have been collected, how should we represent them so that their structure can be understood accurately?

The week begins by reviewing summation notation and then turns to types of variables, scales of measurement, frequency distributions, grouped and ungrouped data, and graphical displays. The assigned readings also introduce central tendency—the mean, median, and mode—as another way to summarize a distribution with a single representative value.

The lecture emphasizes that numerical codes do not automatically determine what mathematical operations are meaningful. The appropriate analysis depends on what the variable actually represents (Sigal, 2026). Privitera (2018, 2024) develops the same general principle through scales of measurement, frequency distributions, and central tendency.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish categorical/discrete variables from continuous/quantitative variables.
  2. Define and recognize the four scales of measurement: nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio.
  3. Explain what information is—and is not—contained in each scale of measurement.
  4. Explain why numerical coding does not necessarily make a variable quantitative.
  5. Construct an ungrouped frequency distribution.
  6. Calculate frequency, proportion/relative frequency, percentage, cumulative frequency, and cumulative relative frequency/percentage.
  7. Explain when grouped frequency distributions are useful.
  8. Calculate a reasonable class interval/bin width, and identify lower and upper limits.
  9. Distinguish apparent limits from real limits.
  10. Choose an appropriate graph for a variable: bar chart, histogram, or frequency polygon.
  11. Explain how graphical design choices can create misleading impressions.
  12. Use summation notation correctly when constants, products, and multiple variables are involved.
  13. Define mean, median, and mode and explain how the appropriate measure of central tendency depends on the data and distribution.
  14. Identify a documented disagreement in the Week 2 materials concerning how Likert-like/rating-scale data should be treated.

Part I — Mathematical Review and Variable Types

1. Review: Summation Notation

Week 2 begins by reinforcing a mathematical skill introduced in Week 1: sigma notation.

The full notation

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \]

means:

Add the values of variable \(X\), beginning with the value indexed \(i=1\) and ending with the value indexed \(i=n\).

The tutorial stresses that the order of the observations matters when the summation limits refer to specific indices. If

\[ X=\{2,6,4,8,1,7\}, \]

then

\[ \sum_{i=3}^{5}X_i = 4+8+1=13. \]

Reordering the scores before applying the index would change the problem and produce the wrong answer (PSYC 210 Teaching Team, 2026a).

Sigma and order of operations

Summation notation interacts with BEDMAS/PEDMAS. Parentheses are therefore essential.

Compare:

\[ \sum X_i + 2 \]

with

\[ \sum(X_i+2). \]

The first expression means “sum the \(X\) values, then add 2.” The second means “add 2 to every included \(X\) value, then sum.”

Likewise,

\[ \left(\sum X_i\right)^2 \]

is different from

\[ \sum X_i^2. \]

The lecture again emphasizes using parentheses to eliminate ambiguity (Sigal, 2026).

2. Three Useful Summation Rules

The Week 2 applied-practice worksheet reinforces three rules.

Rule 1: Sum of combined variables

\[ \sum(X+Y)=\sum X+\sum Y \]

provided the same indexed observations are included.

Rule 2: A multiplicative constant can be factored out

\[ \sum(cX)=c\sum X. \]

For example:

\[ \sum_{i=3}^{6}6Y_i = 6\sum_{i=3}^{6}Y_i. \]

Rule 3: A constant added inside the summation is repeated

If there are \(k\) observations being summed,

\[ \sum(X_i+c)=\sum X_i+kc. \]

This rule is especially easy to misuse. The constant is added once for each observation included in the summation, not once overall.

The worksheet key applies these rules to paired \(X\) and \(Y\) data and also practices products such as \(\sum XY\), squared sums, and combined expressions (PSYC 210 Teaching Team, 2026a).

3. Variables and Why Their Type Matters

A variable can take more than one value. But different variables carry different kinds of information.

The lecture makes a key point:

The way data are analyzed depends on the variables and on what the numerical values actually represent (Sigal, 2026).

A number can function as:

  • a label;
  • a rank;
  • a measurement with equal units;
  • a measurement with equal units and a meaningful zero.

The number itself does not tell you which of these applies. You must know how the variable was defined and measured.

For example, coding:

  • Rock = 1
  • Pop = 2
  • Country = 3
  • Other = 4

does not mean Country is three times Rock. The numbers are labels, not quantities.

This is why scale of measurement matters.

Part II — Scales of Measurement

4. The Four Scales of Measurement

The four scales are often remembered as NOIR:

  1. Nominal
  2. Ordinal
  3. Interval
  4. Ratio

Privitera (2018, 2024) describes these scales in terms of the kinds of numerical information they preserve. The lecture presents the same sequence as increasingly informative forms of measurement (Sigal, 2026).

Scale What it tells us Equal intervals? True zero? Typical example
Nominal Identity/category No No Browser preference
Ordinal Rank/order No No Letter grade, finishing place
Interval Order + equal differences Yes No Celsius temperature
Ratio Order + equal differences + meaningful ratios Yes Yes Height, reaction time

5. Nominal Variables

A nominal variable separates observations into mutually exclusive categories.

The numbers assigned to categories are simply codes.

Examples include:

  • preferred browser;
  • music genre;
  • type of animal;
  • political affiliation.

If browser is coded as:

  • Firefox = 1
  • Safari = 2
  • Chrome = 3

then 3 is not “more browser” than 1. The code identifies the category and nothing more.

What can we meaningfully do?

We can count how many observations fall into each category.

We can therefore report:

  • frequencies;
  • proportions;
  • percentages;
  • the mode.

We cannot interpret arithmetic differences or ratios between category codes.

Important point

A nominal variable can be represented numerically without becoming quantitative. Coding is a data-management choice, not a change in the meaning of the variable (Privitera, 2018, 2024; Sigal, 2026).

6. Ordinal Variables

An ordinal variable contains ordered categories.

Examples include:

  • finishing place in a race;
  • class rank;
  • military rank;
  • letter grades;
  • ordered response categories.

If:

  • 1 = Gold
  • 2 = Silver
  • 3 = Bronze

then Gold is ranked above Bronze, but the numerical difference between 1 and 3 does not tell us how much better Gold is.

Ordinal measurement gives us:

order without known equal distance between ranks.

This is why the median is often a meaningful measure of central tendency for ordinal data (Privitera, 2018, 2024).

7. Interval Variables

An interval variable has ordered values with equal intervals between adjacent units.

The standard example in the Week 2 lecture is Celsius temperature.

The difference between:

  • \(2^\circ C\) and \(3^\circ C\)

represents the same amount of temperature change as the difference between:

  • \(-1^\circ C\) and \(0^\circ C\).

However, Celsius temperature does not have a true zero. \(0^\circ C\) does not mean “no temperature.”

Therefore, ratio statements are not meaningful:

\(10^\circ C\) is not twice as hot as \(5^\circ C\).

Interval variables support meaningful statements about differences, but not arbitrary ratio statements (Privitera, 2018, 2024; Sigal, 2026).

8. Ratio Variables

A ratio variable has all the properties of an interval variable plus a true or meaningful zero.

Examples include:

  • height;
  • weight;
  • reaction time;
  • duration;
  • distance;
  • temperature measured in Kelvin.

If height is measured in metres, \(0\) metres represents the absence of height. Therefore, meaningful ratio comparisons are possible.

For example:

\[ 1.50 / 0.75 = 2. \]

Thus 1.50 metres is twice 0.75 metres.

Ratio measurement is generally the most informative of the four scales because order, differences, and ratios can all be interpreted meaningfully (Privitera, 2018, 2024).

9. Categorical, Discrete, Continuous, Qualitative, and Quantitative

The Week 2 lecture broadly contrasts categorical/discrete variables with continuous/quantitative variables (Sigal, 2026).

Privitera (2018, 2024) makes two separate distinctions:

Discrete vs. continuous

  • Discrete variables consist of separate values or categories.
  • Continuous variables can in principle take values anywhere along a continuum.

Examples:

  • number of siblings → discrete;
  • reaction time → continuous.

Qualitative vs. quantitative

  • Qualitative variables differ by category or class.
  • Quantitative variables differ by amount.

These classifications overlap but are not identical.

For example:

Variable Discrete/continuous Qualitative/quantitative
Browser preference Discrete Qualitative
Number of siblings Discrete Quantitative
Height Continuous Quantitative
Letter grade Discrete Qualitative/ordered

This distinction is useful because “discrete” does not automatically mean “qualitative.”

10. A Source Tension: Likert-Like and Rating Scales

The Week 2 materials contain a genuine disagreement that should not be hidden.

Lecture framing

The lecture treats Likert-like items critically. It questions whether the psychological distance between adjacent response categories is equal and warns against automatically treating such responses as interval measurements (Sigal, 2026).

Privitera framing

Privitera (2024) explicitly describes rating scales as controversial. The text notes that, under strict measurement rules, individual rating-scale responses may be better viewed as ordinal. However, many behavioral researchers pragmatically treat rating-scale values as interval when equal spacing is assumed, especially for statistical analysis (Privitera, 2024).

What should you learn from this?

Do not memorize “Likert = interval” as an unquestionable rule.

Instead ask:

  1. Is this a single ordered response item or a multi-item scale score?
  2. What assumptions are being made about equal distances between response options?
  3. What convention is the instructor using for this course?
  4. Does the planned statistical analysis require stronger measurement assumptions?

For this course, follow the instructor’s stated conventions on assessed work, but understand that the status of rating-scale data is methodologically debated.

Part III — Frequency Distributions and Grouped Data

11. Why Summarize Data?

A raw data set can be technically complete and still be difficult to understand.

Suppose the scores are:

\[ 27,82,32,57,50,61,55,88,57,60. \]

Even with only 10 observations, it takes effort to see:

  • how often each score occurs;
  • whether values cluster in particular regions;
  • whether any values are unusual;
  • what percentage lies below a threshold.

A frequency distribution reorganizes the same observations into a form that makes their pattern easier to interpret (Privitera, 2018, 2024; Sigal, 2026).

A frequency distribution can be displayed as:

  • a table;
  • a bar chart;
  • a histogram;
  • a frequency polygon.

12. Ungrouped Frequency Distributions

An ungrouped frequency distribution lists individual values or categories and the frequency of each one.

A basic table has two columns:

\(X\) \(f\)
value/category number of occurrences

The first consistency check is:

\[ \sum f = n. \]

If the sample contains 10 observations, the frequencies must sum to 10.

Ungrouped distributions are especially useful when:

  • the variable is categorical;
  • there are only a small number of distinct values;
  • retaining the exact observed values is useful.

Privitera (2018, 2024) specifically contrasts this with grouped distributions: ungrouped data preserve individual scores/categories; grouped data combine scores into intervals.

13. Frequency, Proportion, Percentage, and Cumulative Values

A full frequency table can include several related summaries.

Frequency

Frequency \(f\) is the number of observations at a value or in an interval.

Proportion / relative frequency

\[ p=\frac{f}{n}. \]

The proportions should sum to approximately:

\[ \sum p=1.00. \]

Percentage

\[ \%=p\times100. \]

Percentages should sum to approximately:

\[ 100\%. \]

Cumulative frequency

Cumulative frequency is the running total of frequencies.

For an ascending table, it answers:

How many observations scored this value or less?

The final cumulative frequency should equal \(n\).

Cumulative relative frequency / cumulative percentage

A cumulative relative frequency is the running total of proportions.

A cumulative percentage is the running total of percentages.

The final values should be approximately:

\[ 1.00 \]

and

\[ 100\%. \]

respectively (Privitera, 2018, 2024; Sigal, 2026).

14. Worked Frequency Table Example

The lecture uses:

\[ X=\{1,4,1,2,4,2,4\}, \]

so \(n=7\).

The resulting table is:

\(X\) \(f\) \(p\) % CF Cumulative proportion Cumulative %
1 2 .2857 28.57 2 .2857 28.57
2 2 .2857 28.57 4 .5714 57.14
3 0 0 0 4 .5714 57.14
4 3 .4286 42.86 7 1.0000 100.00

Internal checks:

\[ 2+2+0+3=7=n, \]

and the final cumulative values are:

  • CF = 7;
  • cumulative proportion = 1.00;
  • cumulative percentage = 100%.

This example shows why a score with frequency zero may still appear in the table: retaining the full sequence makes the structure of the scale clear (Sigal, 2026).

15. Percentile Rank

Cumulative percentages can be interpreted as percentile ranks.

A percentile rank tells us what percentage of scores fall at or below a specified score under the convention used in the Week 2 materials.

For example, the lecture uses a table of dreams recalled by 50 children. At \(X=2\), the cumulative frequency is 30:

\[ \frac{30}{50}\times100=60\%. \]

Thus a score of 2 corresponds to the 60th percentile in that table (PSYC 210 Teaching Team, 2026b).

Privitera (2018, 2024) distinguishes:

  • percentile point → the score value;
  • percentile rank → the percentage associated with its position in the distribution.

16. Grouped Frequency Distributions

When there are many possible values, listing every score can become unwieldy.

A grouped frequency distribution combines scores into class intervals or bins of equal width (Privitera, 2018, 2024; Sigal, 2026).

Grouping helps us see the overall structure more clearly, but it creates a trade-off:

Grouping simplifies the data, but exact individual values are no longer recoverable from the grouped table.

This is an important form of information loss.

17. Choosing a Bin Width

The lecture uses a practical heuristic of beginning with approximately 10 bins and then adjusting to a meaningful width (Sigal, 2026).

A rough calculation is:

\[ \text{bin width} = \frac{\text{maximum}-\text{minimum}} {\text{number of bins}}. \]

For example, if:

  • maximum = 85;
  • minimum = 29;
  • desired bins = 10,

then:

\[ \frac{85-29}{10} = \frac{56}{10} = 5.6. \]

The lecture simplifies this to a meaningful bin width for the exercise.

Privitera (2018, 2024) presents a closely related but somewhat more formal method using the real range, defined for whole-number scores as one more than the observed difference between maximum and minimum, and recommends roughly 5–20 intervals.

Practical lesson

There is no unique magical bin width. The goal is a display that is:

  • understandable;
  • consistent;
  • non-overlapping;
  • detailed enough to preserve important structure;
  • simple enough to reveal the distribution.

18. Lower Limits, Upper Limits, and Real Limits

Each class interval has a lower and upper limit.

If the interval width is 5, possible apparent intervals are:

  • 25–29
  • 30–34
  • 35–39
  • …

For whole-number data, the apparent width of 25–29 seems to be:

\[ 29-25=4. \]

But the real limits extend half a measurement unit beyond the apparent limits:

\[ 24.5 \text{ to } 29.5. \]

The real width is therefore:

\[ 29.5-24.5=5. \]

This distinction matters when constructing continuous histograms because adjacent intervals meet at real boundaries without gaps (Sigal, 2026).

19. Worked Example: SAT-Type Scores

The Lecture 2 worksheet uses data modeled closely on Katz et al. (1990). Participants answered SAT-type questions without seeing the passage on which the questions were based.

The no-passage scores range from 34 to 57. Students are asked to:

  1. construct an ungrouped frequency table;
  2. calculate cumulative frequency and cumulative relative frequency;
  3. convert the data to a grouped frequency table;
  4. draw a histogram;
  5. compare these scores with a second group that read the passage (Sigal, 2026).

The answer key shows that the no-passage sample has \(n=28\), while the passage group has \(n=17\). The passage-group scores are generally higher, but the worksheet deliberately separates this descriptive observation from the later question of whether a difference is statistically significant.

That distinction is essential:

A graph or frequency table can show an apparent difference. Inferential statistics are needed to evaluate whether the evidence supports a broader population-level conclusion.

Part IV — Visualization and Central Tendency

20. Visualizing Frequency Distributions

The lecture emphasizes three basic graphical displays:

  1. Bar charts
  2. Histograms
  3. Frequency polygons

The correct choice depends on the type of variable and on what the display is intended to communicate (Sigal, 2026).

21. Bar Charts

A bar chart is typically used for discrete or categorical data.

Key visual feature

The bars do not touch.

The gaps remind us that the categories are distinct rather than positions along a continuous measurement scale.

For nominal data, categories may often be reordered without changing their meaning.

For ordinal data, the order should usually be preserved because the rank itself is meaningful.

22. Histograms

A histogram is used for quantitative continuous data.

Key visual feature

Adjacent bars touch.

The touching bars communicate that values lie along a continuum.

Privitera (2018, 2024) gives three core rules:

  1. bar height represents the frequency in each interval;
  2. bar bases correspond to interval boundaries;
  3. adjacent bars touch because the variable is continuous.

Histograms are therefore fundamentally different from bar charts, even though both use rectangular bars.

23. Frequency Polygons

A frequency polygon represents a distribution using points connected by lines rather than bars.

It conveys the same basic information as a histogram but can make the overall shape of a distribution particularly easy to see.

Frequency polygons are appropriate for continuous quantitative data (Privitera, 2018; Sigal, 2026).

24. What Grouping Changes

The lecture explicitly contrasts grouped and ungrouped displays:

  • Ungrouped distributions retain all distinct observed values.
  • Grouped distributions summarize observations into intervals.
  • Once grouped, the exact original observations are no longer recoverable from the display (Sigal, 2026).

This is not an error. It is the cost of summarization.

The analyst therefore has to decide how much detail to preserve.

25. Graphs Can Clarify—and Mislead

A graph is not automatically neutral simply because it is based on real numbers.

The Week 2 lecture demonstrates how changing the vertical-axis range can make a small difference between groups appear dramatic or modest.

A responsible graph should therefore:

  • have clearly labeled axes;
  • use an appropriate scale;
  • make truncation explicit;
  • use clear titles and captions;
  • include an informative legend when needed;
  • avoid visual emphasis that exceeds what the data support (Sigal, 2026).

The larger lesson is the same as Week 1:

A statistically correct number can still be communicated in a misleading way.

26. Central Tendency: A Second Way to Summarize Data

The assigned Chapter 3 readings extend the logic of summarization.

A frequency distribution summarizes how scores are distributed.

A measure of central tendency reduces the distribution to a single value near its center (Privitera, 2018, 2024).

The three main measures are:

  • mean;
  • median;
  • mode.

These measures are not interchangeable. Each answers a slightly different question.

27. The Mean

The arithmetic mean is:

\[ M=\frac{\sum X}{n} \]

for a sample, or

\[ \mu=\frac{\sum X}{N} \]

for a population.

The calculation is the same; the notation changes because one value describes a sample and the other a population (Privitera, 2018, 2024).

The mean is often described as the balance point of a distribution.

Important characteristic

Because every observation contributes to the mean, unusual or extreme scores can shift it substantially.

The mean is typically most informative for approximately symmetrical distributions of interval- or ratio-scale data (Privitera, 2018, 2024).

28. The Median

The median is the middle score after scores are ordered.

Its position is:

\[ \frac{n+1}{2}. \]

For an odd number of observations, the median is the single middle score.

For an even number of observations, average the two middle scores.

The median is resistant to extreme values. Privitera (2018, 2024) therefore recommends it particularly for:

  • skewed distributions;
  • ordinal data;
  • distributions containing important outliers.

The median also corresponds to the 50th percentile.

29. The Mode

The mode is the value or category occurring most frequently.

A distribution can be:

  • unimodal;
  • bimodal;
  • multimodal;
  • nonmodal.

The mode has an important advantage:

It can be used with nominal data, for which a mean or median may not be meaningful.

For example, the most common preferred browser is meaningful; the “average browser code” is not.

30. Choosing Mean, Median, or Mode

The readings summarize the choice as follows (Privitera, 2018, 2024):

Measure Often appropriate when…
Mean Data are interval/ratio and reasonably symmetrical
Median Data are ordinal or skewed; outliers matter
Mode Data are nominal or the most frequent category/value is substantively important

These are guiding principles rather than mechanical rules. The appropriate summary depends on the research question, measurement scale, and shape of the distribution.

Part V — Integration, Review, and Practice

31. How the Concepts Fit Together

Week 2 can be understood as one sequence:

What kind of variable do I have?

↓

What does the measurement scale allow me to say?

↓

Should I preserve individual scores or group them?

↓

What frequency summaries are useful?

↓

What graph is appropriate?

↓

Would a measure of central tendency add useful information?

↓

Does the display accurately communicate the evidence?

The scale of measurement affects what mathematical summaries are meaningful. The frequency distribution shows the structure of the data. Graphs make that structure visible. Measures of central tendency compress it further into representative values.

33. Common Misunderstandings

“If categories are coded with numbers, they become quantitative.”

No. The meaning of the numbers depends on the measurement process. Nominal codes remain labels.

“Ordinal numbers tell me how far apart categories are.”

No. Ordinal data provide order, not equal distance.

“Zero always means none.”

No. A true zero is a property of ratio scales. \(0^\circ C\) is not an absence of temperature.

“Grouped frequency distributions are more accurate.”

They are often clearer, but they are less precise because individual values are hidden within intervals.

“All frequency tables should omit zero-frequency values.”

Not necessarily. Including missing internal values can make the scale and cumulative pattern clearer.

“Bar charts and histograms are the same thing.”

No. The gaps between bars in a bar chart communicate discrete categories; touching histogram bars represent continuity.

“A graph cannot mislead if all the numbers are correct.”

False. Axis limits, scales, binning, and graphical emphasis can substantially change the viewer’s impression.

“The mean is always the best description of the center.”

No. Outliers and skew can make the median more representative; nominal data often require the mode.

“Likert-like scales are obviously interval.”

The Week 2 sources do not agree on this. The lecture is strongly critical of that assumption, while Privitera (2024) explains why many behavioral researchers pragmatically treat rating scales as interval. Know the course convention and the methodological issue.

34. Tutorial Preparation

A TA should be ready to move repeatedly between conceptual interpretation and calculation.

Questions students may ask

“Why is grade ordinal but height ratio?”

Grade categories have order but unequal or undefined distances between adjacent grades. Height has equal units and a meaningful zero.

“Why include a score with zero frequency?”

Because a frequency table often represents the structure of a scale, not merely the values observed. Omitting internal values can obscure gaps.

“How do I know whether to group data?”

Grouping becomes useful when many distinct values make an ungrouped table hard to read. But grouping sacrifices exact detail.

“Why do histogram bars touch?”

Because the variable is treated as continuous, and adjacent intervals meet at their real boundaries.

“How do I know which direction to calculate cumulative frequency?”

It depends on the question. Bottom-up cumulative summaries naturally answer “at or below” questions; top-down summaries answer “at or above” questions (Privitera, 2018, 2024).

“Which measure of central tendency should I use?”

Ask about both the measurement scale and the distribution:

  • nominal → mode;
  • ordinal or strongly skewed → median is often useful;
  • interval/ratio and roughly symmetrical → mean is often useful.

35. Practice Questions

Measurement Scales

  1. Preferred web browser: nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio?
  2. Finishing position in a marathon: nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio?
  3. Celsius temperature: nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio?
  4. Reaction time in milliseconds: nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio?
  5. Explain why coding categories 1, 2, 3, and 4 does not automatically justify calculating their mean.

Frequency Tables

For:

\[ X=\{2,4,4,5,5,5,7,8,8,9\}, \]

construct a table containing:

  • \(X\);
  • \(f\);
  • \(p\);
  • percentage;
  • cumulative frequency;
  • cumulative percentage.

Check that:

\[ \sum f=n, \]

\[ \sum p=1, \]

and percentages sum to 100%.

Grouping

For scores ranging from 12 to 92:

  1. Calculate the observed range.
  2. Estimate a bin width for approximately 10 bins.
  3. Propose a set of non-overlapping class intervals.
  4. Explain what information is lost after grouping.

Graph Choice

Choose the most appropriate basic display and explain why:

  1. favorite music genre;
  2. course letter grade;
  3. reaction time;
  4. age in years measured continuously;
  5. number of children in a household.

Central Tendency

For:

\[ 2,3,4,5,6,6,100 \]

calculate:

  1. mean;
  2. median;
  3. mode.

Then explain which value is most representative and why.

36. What You Should Be Able to Explain

Before moving on, you should be able to explain in your own words:

  • why the scale of measurement matters;
  • what information each of NOIR preserves;
  • why numeric coding is not the same as quantitative measurement;
  • why nominal and ordinal data are different;

39. Week 2 Takeaway

Week 2 is fundamentally about matching the summary to the data.

Before calculating anything, ask:

What kind of variable is this, and what do its values actually mean?

Then ask:

What representation—table, grouped distribution, graph, mean, median, or mode—preserves the information I need without implying more than the data support?

That habit is more important than memorizing any single formula.

Part VI — Midterm Review Guide

The official Lecture 2 midterm guide emphasizes categorical versus continuous variables, the NOIR scales of measurement, frequency distributions, grouped frequency distributions, and graphical representations of data. The main computations are frequencies, proportions, percentages, cumulative frequencies, and cumulative percentages.

The Big Picture

Week 2 asks two linked questions:

What kind of variable do I have?
How should I summarize and display it?

The measurement scale determines what the values mean, and that helps determine which summaries and graphs are appropriate.

Variable Types and NOIR

Categorical vs. continuous

Categorical variables represent qualitative categories or ordered categories.

Continuous variables represent measured quantities using a defined unit.

Nominal

Nominal values distinguish categories only.

Examples:

  • psychiatric diagnosis;
  • dominant hand;
  • eye colour.

There is no meaningful rank order.

Ordinal

Ordinal values have a meaningful order, but the distance between adjacent categories is not guaranteed to be equal.

Examples:

  • Mild, Moderate, Severe;
  • class rank;
  • letter grades.

Interval

Interval variables have equal measurement units but an arbitrary zero point.

Examples in the guide include Celsius temperature, standardized IQ scores, and calendar years.

Ratio

Ratio variables have equal units and a meaningful zero indicating absence of the measured quantity.

Examples include:

  • reaction time;
  • distance;
  • mass;
  • counts.

A useful exam reminder is that the same phenomenon can be measured on different scales depending on how it is operationalized.

Frequency Distributions

A frequency distribution compresses raw observations into an organized table.

The key columns are:

  • \(f\): frequency;
  • proportion: \(f/N\);
  • percentage: \((f/N)\times100\);
  • CF: cumulative frequency;
  • cumulative percentage or cumulative relative frequency.

Think of cumulative frequency as:

“How many observations are at this value or less?”

The final cumulative frequency must equal \(N\), and the final cumulative percentage must equal 100%.

Apparent and Real Limits

A displayed whole-number score such as 21 has:

  • apparent value = 21;
  • lower real limit = 20.5;
  • upper real limit = 21.5.

This becomes important for grouped distributions and percentile calculations later.

Grouped Frequency Distributions

When the range is large, scores can be combined into class intervals.

The official formula sheet gives:

\[ \text{Bin Width} = \frac{\text{Maximum}-\text{Minimum}} {\text{Number of Bins}}. \]

The course guide recommends aiming for roughly 10 bins and then choosing a convenient meaningful width.

Choosing the Correct Graph

Bar chart

Use for categorical data.

Bars do not touch.

Histogram

Use for continuous data.

Bars touch.

Frequency polygon

Use for continuous data; plotted points are connected across class-interval midpoints.

Good visualizations should also use clear labels, appropriate scales, and avoid unnecessary visual clutter or misleading truncation.

Formula-Sheet Connection

For Week 2, the main official formula is:

\[ \text{Bin Width} = \frac{\max-\min}{\#\text{ of bins}}. \]

You should also be able to calculate proportions and percentages even though these are simple arithmetic rather than a separate boxed formula.
