Week 04 — Probability, Normal Distributions, and Standard Scores

Overview

Week 4 moves from descriptive statistics toward the foundations of inferential statistics.

The central question is:

How can probability help us describe what outcomes are likely, unlikely, typical, or unusual?

The two supplied editions of Privitera organize this material differently.

  • The 2018 second edition combines introductory probability with the normal distribution, the standard normal distribution, z scores, the unit normal table, and the use of z scores to locate probabilities and cutoff scores.
  • The 2024 fourth edition expands probability itself much more extensively. It covers relative frequency, mutually exclusive, independent, complementary, and conditional outcomes, Bayes’s theorem, probability distributions, expected value, and the variance and standard deviation of probability distributions, including the binomial distribution.

This Study Guide therefore treats the two editions as complementary rather than pretending that they have the same Chapter 5 structure.

The broad conceptual sequence is:

Probability

↓

Relative frequency and relationships among outcomes

↓

Probability distributions and expected value

↓

Normal distributions

↓

Standardization with z scores

↓

Using areas under the normal curve to locate probabilities and cutoff scores

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. Define probability and explain when probability is useful.
  2. Distinguish a random event from a fixed event.
  3. Define sample space and compute a simple probability.
  4. Explain the relationship between probability and relative frequency.
  5. Distinguish mutually exclusive, independent, complementary, and conditional outcomes.
  6. Apply the additive and multiplicative rules in appropriate situations.
  7. Interpret conditional probability notation such as (p(AB)).
  8. Explain the logic of Bayes’s theorem at an introductory level.
  9. Define a random variable and a probability distribution.
  10. Explain why probabilities in a probability distribution must sum to 1.
  11. Compute and interpret an expected value.
  12. Explain the variance and standard deviation of a probability distribution.
  13. Explain the basic logic of a binomial distribution.
  14. Identify the main characteristics of a normal distribution.
  15. Explain the relationship between area under the normal curve and probability.
  16. Define the standard normal distribution.
  17. Compute and interpret a z score.
  18. Use a unit normal table conceptually to locate areas above, below, or between scores.
  19. Work backward from a probability or percentile to a raw-score cutoff.
  20. Explain how standardization allows scores from different distributions to be compared.

Part I — Foundations of Probability

1. What Is Probability?

Probability describes the likelihood that an outcome will occur.

In the simplest case:

\[ p(x)=\frac{f(x)}{\text{sample space}}, \]

where:

  • (p(x)) = probability of outcome (x);
  • (f(x)) = frequency of outcome (x);
  • sample space = total number of possible outcomes.

Both editions emphasize that probability is useful when outcomes can vary from one observation to the next (Privitera, 2018, 2024).

Example: one fair coin

The sample space is:

\[ \{H,T\}. \]

Therefore:

\[ p(H)=\frac{1}{2}=0.50. \]

2. Random Events and Fixed Events

A random event is one for which the observed outcome can vary.

Examples:

  • flipping a coin;
  • selecting a person from a population;
  • whether a flight is delayed;
  • whether a participant responds correctly.

A fixed event is one for which the outcome does not vary.

Probability is useful for predicting random events because their outcomes are uncertain before observation (Privitera, 2018, 2024).

3. The Sample Space

The sample space is the complete set of possible outcomes.

For one coin:

\[ S=\{H,T\}. \]

For two fair coins:

\[ S=\{HH,HT,TH,TT\}. \]

Thus:

\[ |S|=4. \]

The probability of two heads is:

\[ p(HH)=\frac{1}{4}=0.25. \]

A common mistake is to count only the outcomes of interest rather than all possible outcomes.

4. Basic Rules of Probability

A valid probability must satisfy:

\[ 0\le p\le1. \]

Probability can be expressed as:

  • a fraction;
  • a decimal;
  • a percentage;
  • a proportion.

Examples:

\[ \frac{1}{2}=0.50=50\%. \]

A probability cannot be negative and cannot exceed 1 (Privitera, 2018, 2024).

5. Probability and Relative Frequency

The 2024 edition makes the connection especially explicit:

A relative frequency is the probability of an outcome’s occurrence when frequencies are distributed across all possible outcomes (Privitera, 2024).

For two fair coins:

Outcome (f(x)) (p(x))
HH 1 .25
HT or TH 2 .50
TT 1 .25

The frequencies sum to the sample space:

\[ 1+2+1=4. \]

The probabilities sum to:

\[ 0.25+0.50+0.25=1.00. \]

This connection links Week 4 directly back to the frequency distributions introduced earlier in the course.

Part II — Relationships Among Outcomes

6. Mutually Exclusive Outcomes

Two outcomes are mutually exclusive when they cannot occur together.

For one coin flip, heads and tails are mutually exclusive.

Therefore:

\[ p(H\cap T)=0. \]

If two outcomes are mutually exclusive, the probability of either one occurring is found with the additive rule:

\[ p(A\cup B)=p(A)+p(B). \]

This rule applies only when the outcomes cannot overlap (Privitera, 2024).

7. Independent Outcomes

Two outcomes are independent when the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other.

For two independent outcomes:

\[ p(A\cap B)=p(A)p(B). \]

Example:

If:

\[ p(A)=0.80 \]

and:

\[ p(B)=0.80, \]

then:

\[ p(A\cap B)=0.80(0.80)=0.64. \]

The 2024 text uses this logic to show how repeated independent selections combine multiplicatively.

8. Complementary Outcomes

Two outcomes are complementary when together they exhaust all possible outcomes.

Thus:

\[ p(A)+p(B)=1. \]

Therefore:

\[ p(A)=1-p(B). \]

Examples include:

  • pass / fail;
  • win / lose, assuming no tie;
  • heads / tails.

Complementary probabilities are often useful when it is easier to calculate the probability of the opposite outcome.

9. Conditional Outcomes

Two outcomes are conditional when the probability of one outcome depends on another outcome.

Conditional probability is written:

\[ p(A\mid B), \]

read as:

the probability of (A), given that (B) occurred.

The basic conditional-probability formula is:

\[ p(A\mid B) = \frac{p(A\cap B)}{p(B)}. \]

The key conceptual change is the sample space.

Once we condition on (B), we no longer consider all cases—only the cases for which (B) is true (Privitera, 2024).

10. Worked Conditional-Probability Example

The 2024 edition uses a hypothetical table of 200 mothers classified by hospital type and insurance status.

For women giving birth in public hospitals:

  • 130 gave birth in public hospitals;
  • 80 of those were uninsured.

Therefore:

\[ p(\text{Uninsured}\mid \text{Public}) = \frac{80}{130} \approx0.62. \]

This differs from:

\[ p(\text{Uninsured}\cap \text{Public}) = \frac{80}{200} = 0.40. \]

The first is conditional because the sample space has been restricted to the public-hospital group.

11. Bayes’s Theorem

The 2024 edition extends conditional probability with Bayes’s theorem:

\[ p(A\mid B) = \frac{p(B\mid A)p(A)}{p(B)}. \]

Bayes’s theorem relates:

  • a conditional probability;
  • the reverse conditional probability;
  • the marginal probability of (A);
  • the marginal probability of (B).

At this stage, the main goal is conceptual:

Bayes’s theorem updates the probability of one outcome after taking another observed condition into account.

This becomes especially important later in statistical inference (Privitera, 2024).

Part III — Probability Distributions and Expected Value

12. Random Variables

A random variable represents the possible numerical outcomes of a random process.

Examples:

  • number of errors on a test;
  • number of symptoms;
  • number of items remembered;
  • number of successful trials.

A random variable is not a single observed value. It represents the set of possible outcomes that could occur (Privitera, 2024).

13. Probability Distributions

A probability distribution lists the possible outcomes of a random variable and the probability of each outcome.

A valid probability distribution satisfies:

\[ 0\le p(x)\le1 \]

for every outcome, and:

\[ \sum p(x)=1. \]

Example:

(x) 1 2 3 4 5
(p(x)) .250 .350 .175 .125 .100

Check:

\[ .250+.350+.175+.125+.100=1.00. \]

14. Expected Value

The expected value is the mean of a probability distribution.

It is:

\[ \mu=\sum xp(x). \]

For each possible outcome:

  1. multiply the outcome value by its probability;
  2. sum the products.

The expected value is sometimes called the long-term mean because it describes the average outcome expected across a very large number of repetitions (Privitera, 2024).

15. Expected Value Example

Suppose the probabilities of forgetting 0, 1, 2, or 3 items are:

Items forgotten Probability
0 .24
1 .16
2 .40
3 .20

Then:

\[ \mu = 0(.24)+1(.16)+2(.40)+3(.20). \]

So:

\[ \mu=1.56. \]

The interpretation is not that a person can literally forget 1.56 items.

It means that across many repetitions, the long-term average would approach 1.56 items (Privitera, 2024).

16. Variance of a Probability Distribution

The 2024 edition extends Week 3’s variance idea to probability distributions.

The definitional formula is:

\[ \sigma^2 = \sum (x-\mu)^2p(x). \]

This is a probability-weighted version of squared deviation from the mean.

The standard deviation is:

\[ \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}. \]

Thus probability distributions also have:

  • a center;
  • a spread.

17. Binomial Probability Distributions

A binomial distribution applies when the random variable has two possible outcomes.

Examples:

  • success / failure;
  • yes / no;
  • remembered / forgotten;
  • heads / tails.

If:

  • (n) = number of trials;
  • (p) = probability of the outcome of interest;
  • (q=1-p),

then:

\[ \mu=np, \]

\[ \sigma^2=npq, \]

and:

\[ \sigma=\sqrt{npq}. \]

The 2024 edition uses these formulas as shortcuts for two-outcome probability distributions.

Part IV — Normal Distributions

18. Why the Normal Distribution Matters

The 2018 edition connects probability directly to the normal distribution.

Many behavioral variables are treated as approximately normal because:

  • most observations cluster near the center;
  • relatively few observations occur at the extremes.

This allows probability to be represented as area under a smooth curve (Privitera, 2018).

The normal distribution is also called:

  • Gaussian;
  • bell-shaped;
  • symmetrical.

19. Eight Characteristics of the Normal Distribution

The 2018 edition identifies eight characteristics.

  1. The normal distribution is mathematically defined.
  2. It is theoretical.
  3. Mean, median, and mode are located together at the 50th percentile.
  4. It is symmetrical.
  5. The mean can take any value.
  6. The standard deviation can take any positive value.
  7. Total area under the curve equals 1.00.
  8. The tails are asymptotic.

These characteristics explain why the normal curve is useful for probability calculations (Privitera, 2018).

20. Symmetry

In a normal distribution:

\[ M=Md=Mo. \]

Half the area lies below the mean:

\[ 0.50, \]

and half lies above:

\[ 0.50. \]

Because the curve is symmetrical, equal distances above and below the mean correspond to equal areas.

This symmetry greatly simplifies probability calculations.

21. Area Under the Curve

For a normal distribution:

\[ \text{total area}=1.00. \]

Thus area corresponds directly to probability.

For example:

\[ 0.50 \]

of the area lies on either side of the mean.

The 2018 text connects this to the empirical rule introduced earlier:

  • about 68% within 1 SD;
  • about 95% within 2 SD;
  • about 99.7% within 3 SD.

22. Asymptotic Tails

The tails of a normal curve approach the horizontal axis but never touch it.

This means that:

  • extremely unusual scores remain theoretically possible;
  • probability becomes very small in the tails;
  • the curve extends indefinitely in both directions.

23. The Statistical Norm

The 2018 edition introduces the idea of the statistical norm.

Approximately 95% of normally distributed scores lie within:

\[ \pm2SD \]

of the mean.

Scores farther than roughly 2 SD from the mean are statistically less common.

This is a statistical definition of unusualness, not a judgment about whether a person or behavior is socially, clinically, or morally “normal” (Privitera, 2018).

Part V — Standard Normal Distribution and z Scores

24. Why Standardize?

Normal distributions can have different means and standard deviations.

For example:

  • Exam A: (M=70, SD=5)
  • Exam B: (M=80, SD=10)

A raw score of 85 does not have the same relative meaning in both distributions.

Standardization solves this problem by expressing raw scores in standard deviation units.

25. The Standard Normal Distribution

The standard normal distribution has:

\[ \mu=0 \]

and:

\[ \sigma=1. \]

Scores on the horizontal axis are expressed as z scores.

A z score tells us how many standard deviations a score lies above or below the mean (Privitera, 2018).

26. The z Transformation

For a population:

\[ z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}. \]

For a sample:

\[ z=\frac{x-M}{SD}. \]

Interpretation:

  • (z=0): at the mean;
  • (z=+1): one SD above the mean;
  • (z=-1): one SD below the mean;
  • (z=+2): two SD above the mean.

The sign indicates direction; the magnitude indicates distance.

27. Worked z-Score Example

Suppose:

\[ M=12,\quad SD=2,\quad x=14. \]

Then:

\[ z = \frac{14-12}{2} = 1.00. \]

So a raw score of 14 is:

one standard deviation above the mean.

This is the central meaning of a z score (Privitera, 2018).

28. z Scores Preserve Relative Position

Suppose two people have different raw scores in different distributions.

A z score allows us to compare how unusual each score is relative to its own distribution.

This is why standard scores are useful when:

  • variables use different measurement units;
  • different tests have different means and standard deviations;
  • researchers want a common scale.

Part VI — Using the Unit Normal Table

29. What the z Table Represents

The 2018 edition introduces the unit normal table, or z table.

The table links z scores to areas under the standard normal curve.

In the text’s table:

  • Column A lists z scores;
  • Column B lists area between the mean and z;
  • Column C lists area from z toward the tail.

Because the normal curve is symmetric, areas for positive and negative z scores mirror one another.

30. Two Steps for Locating a Probability

To locate the probability of a raw score in a normal distribution:

Step 1

Convert the raw score to a z score.

Step 2

Use the z table to find the relevant area.

This general strategy applies whether the question asks for a probability:

  • above a score;
  • below a score;
  • between two scores (Privitera, 2018).

31. Probability Above a Score

Suppose:

\[ M=8,\quad SD=2,\quad x=12. \]

Then:

\[ z=\frac{12-8}{2}=2. \]

The tail area beyond:

\[ z=2.00 \]

is approximately:

\[ p=.0228. \]

So the probability of a score greater than 12 is about 2.28% (Privitera, 2018).

32. Probability Below a Score

Suppose a raw score converts to:

\[ z=0.50. \]

The area between the mean and (z=0.50) is:

\[ 0.1915. \]

Because 0.5000 lies below the mean:

\[ p(X<x) = 0.5000+0.1915 = 0.6915. \]

The diagram on page 29 of the 2018 chapter visually shows this logic: the shaded region includes the entire lower half of the curve plus the area between the mean and (z=.50).

33. Probability in a Tail Below the Mean

Suppose:

\[ z=-0.92. \]

By symmetry, use the same tail area as:

\[ z=+0.92. \]

The 2018 example gives:

\[ p=.1788. \]

The negative sign identifies which side of the mean the score lies on; it does not imply a negative probability.

34. Probability Between Two Scores

Suppose one score is:

\[ z=-1 \]

and the other is:

\[ z=+1. \]

The area between the mean and (z=1) is:

\[ 0.3413. \]

By symmetry:

\[ 0.3413+0.3413=0.6826. \]

Thus approximately 68.26% of scores lie between (z=-1) and (z=+1).

This matches the familiar empirical-rule approximation.

Part VII — Working Backward From Probability to Scores

35. From Percentile to z Score

Sometimes the probability is known and the raw cutoff is unknown.

For example:

What raw score defines the top 10%?

The procedure reverses the earlier steps.

Step 1

Use the z table to identify the z score for the target tail probability.

Step 2

Convert the z score back into a raw score.

36. Solving for a Raw Score

Starting with:

\[ z=\frac{x-M}{SD}, \]

solve for (x):

\[ x=M+z(SD). \]

If:

\[ M=100,\quad SD=15,\quad z=1.28, \]

then:

\[ x = 100+1.28(15) = 119.2. \]

Thus approximately 119.2 is the cutoff for the top 10% in that distribution (Privitera, 2018).

37. Bottom-Tail Cutoffs

For a bottom-tail cutoff, the z score is negative.

If the bottom 25% corresponds approximately to:

\[ z=-0.675, \]

and:

\[ M=100,\quad SD=15, \]

then:

\[ x = 100+(-0.675)(15) = 89.875. \]

Rounded:

\[ x\approx89.88. \]

The sign of z tells us whether the cutoff lies above or below the mean.

39. Conceptual Bridge to Inferential Statistics

These topics connect directly to later inferential statistics.

Probability allows us to ask:

How likely is an outcome?

Probability distributions allow us to ask:

How likely is each possible value?

Standardization allows us to ask:

How unusual is this score relative to its distribution?

Normal-curve areas allow us to ask:

What proportion of scores should fall beyond this point?

Later hypothesis tests build on exactly this logic.

Part X — Common Misunderstandings

41. Probability Misunderstandings

“If there are two possible outcomes, each must have probability .50.”

No. Two outcomes can have unequal probabilities.

“A probability can be negative.”

No.

“If two outcomes cannot both occur, they are independent.”

No. Mutually exclusive and independent mean different things.

“Conditional probability uses the same denominator as ordinary probability.”

Not necessarily. Conditioning changes the sample space.

“Expected value is what must happen next.”

No. It is a long-term average, not a prediction of the next single observation.

42. Normal-Distribution Misunderstandings

“Normal means socially or clinically normal.”

No. Here, normal refers to a mathematical distribution.

“All real data are exactly normal.”

No. The normal distribution is theoretical; empirical data may approximate it.

“A z score of −2 means a negative raw score.”

No. It means the score is two standard deviations below the mean.

“A negative z score means negative probability.”

No. Probabilities remain between 0 and 1.

“A higher raw score always means better relative performance.”

Not across different distributions. Relative standing depends on the mean and standard deviation.

Part XIV — Week 4 Takeaway

52. Week 4 Takeaway

Probability provides the bridge from describing observed data to reasoning about what is likely or unlikely.

The core logic is:

Probability quantifies uncertainty.

The 2024 reading develops that logic through outcome relationships, conditional probability, probability distributions, expected value, and binomial models.

The 2018 reading connects the same logic to the normal distribution and shows how standard scores translate raw observations into a common scale.

The most important conceptual move is this:

A raw score becomes much more informative when we know where it falls relative to a distribution.

That idea—especially through z scores and probability—will become central to inferential statistics in later weeks.
