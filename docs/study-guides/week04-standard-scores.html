Week 04 — Standard Scores, Normal Distributions, and Probability

Overview

Week 4 builds directly on Week 3.

Last week, you learned how to describe a distribution using:

  • measures of central tendency;
  • measures of shape;
  • measures of variability.

This week asks the next question:

How can we describe where one particular score sits relative to the rest of a distribution?

The lecture introduces standard scores, especially z scores, as a way to combine information about the center and spread of a distribution into a single interpretable number. A z score tells us whether a raw score is above or below its mean and how many standard deviations away from the mean it lies (Sigal, 2026).

The lecture then connects z scores to the normal distribution and the standard normal distribution, where the mean is 0 and the standard deviation is 1. Once scores are standardized, the z table allows us to translate locations in the distribution into proportions and probabilities (Sigal, 2026; Privitera, 2018).

The lecture’s main intellectual sequence is therefore:

Center + spread

↓

Standard score

↓

z score

↓

Standard normal distribution

↓

z table

↓

Probability / percentile interpretation

A crucial point from the lecture is that standardizing scores does not make a skewed distribution normal. Standardization changes the scale, not the shape (Sigal, 2026).

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. Review population and sample variance and standard deviation.
  2. Explain why sample variance uses (n-1).
  3. Explain what a standard score is.
  4. Explain why a raw score is difficult to interpret without its mean and standard deviation.
  5. Define a z score as a particular standardized score.
  6. Calculate a z score from a raw score.
  7. Interpret the sign and magnitude of a z score.
  8. Identify which z score is closest to the mean.
  9. Explain the defining properties of the standard normal distribution.
  10. Explain how a normal distribution is defined by its mean and standard deviation.
  11. Describe the main properties of a normal distribution.
  12. Explain the approximate 3-sigma rule.
  13. Use the z table conceptually to identify areas between the mean and z and areas beyond z.
  14. Convert probabilities or percentile ranks back into z scores.
  15. Convert z scores back into raw scores.
  16. Convert scores from one standardized metric to another.
  17. Explain why standardization allows comparison across differently scaled variables.
  18. Explain why converting to z does not normalize a non-normal distribution.
  19. Solve the Week 4 worksheet problems involving skew, z scores, and normal-curve proportions.
  20. Distinguish standard scores in general from z scores specifically.

Part I — Review: Variability and Degrees of Freedom

1. Population Variance and Standard Deviation

The lecture begins with a direct review of Week 3.

For a population:

\[ \sigma^2 = \frac{\sum (x_i-\mu)^2}{N} \]

and:

\[ \sigma = \sqrt{ \frac{\sum (x_i-\mu)^2}{N} }. \]

The lecture uses:

\[ X=\{1,0,6,1\}, \]

with:

\[ \mu=2. \]

Then:

\[ SS = (1-2)^2+(0-2)^2+(6-2)^2+(1-2)^2 = 22. \]

Thus:

\[ \sigma^2 = \frac{22}{4} = 5.5, \]

and:

\[ \sigma = \sqrt{5.5} \approx 2.35 \]

(Sigal, 2026).

2. Sum of Squares

The lecture explicitly names:

\[ \sum (x_i-\mu)^2 \]

and:

\[ \sum (x_i-\bar{x})^2 \]

as the sum of squares (SS).

Because deviations are squared:

  • SS can never be negative;
  • SS equals 0 only if every observation is identical.

If all observations are identical, there is no variation, so variance and standard deviation are also 0 (Sigal, 2026).

3. Sample Variance and Standard Deviation

For a sample:

\[ s^2 = \frac{\sum (x_i-\bar{x})^2}{n-1} = \frac{SS}{df}. \]

The standard deviation is:

\[ s = \sqrt{ \frac{SS}{n-1} }. \]

Using the same data:

\[ SS=22,\quad n=4, \]

so:

\[ s^2 = \frac{22}{3} = 7.33, \]

and:

\[ s = \sqrt{7.33} \approx 2.71 \]

(Sigal, 2026).

4. Why (n-1)?

The lecture gives two complementary explanations.

Estimation explanation

Samples do not fully represent populations, especially at the rare and extreme ends. As a result, sample spread tends to underestimate population spread. Dividing by (n-1) instead of (n) makes the variance estimate larger and improves its performance as an estimate of population variance (Sigal, 2026).

Degrees-of-freedom explanation

Degrees of freedom also refers to the number of values that are independent and “free to vary.”

If the mean and all but one score are known, the final score is determined.

Thus:

\[ df=n-1. \]

Part II — Why Standard Scores?

5. The Problem With Raw Scores

A raw score in isolation is difficult to interpret.

A score such as:

\[ X=53 \]

could be:

  • low;
  • average;
  • high;

depending on the distribution.

To interpret a raw score, we need to know:

  • the center;
  • the spread.

The lecture frames this as the core motivation for standard scores (Sigal, 2026).

6. What Is a Standard Score?

A standard score combines information about:

  • center;
  • spread;

into a single number.

More formally, the lecture describes a standard score as a mean deviation standardized by an average measure of variability (Sigal, 2026).

A standard score tells us two things:

  1. Direction
    Is the score above or below the mean?

  2. Distance
    How many standard-deviation units separate the score from the mean?

7. Standard Scores as Relative Position

A standard score re-expresses a raw score in terms of the distribution it belongs to.

This is important because:

\[ X=80 \]

does not have the same meaning in every distribution.

If one class has:

\[ M=70,\quad SD=5, \]

then:

\[ X=80 \]

is:

\[ z=\frac{80-70}{5}=2. \]

That is two standard deviations above the mean.

But if another class has:

\[ M=80,\quad SD=10, \]

then the same raw score corresponds to:

\[ z=0. \]

The raw score is identical, but the relative standing is not.

Part III — z Scores

8. What Is a z Score?

A z score is a specific kind of standard score.

The lecture defines z scores as a parameterization that transforms a distribution to:

\[ \mu=0 \]

and:

\[ \sigma=1. \]

For a population:

\[ z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}. \]

For a sample:

\[ z=\frac{X-\bar{x}}{s}. \]

A z score is therefore a raw deviation divided by a standard deviation (Sigal, 2026).

9. Interpreting the Sign of z

The sign tells us direction.

Positive

\[ z>0 \]

means the score is above the mean.

Negative

\[ z<0 \]

means the score is below the mean.

Zero

\[ z=0 \]

means the score is exactly at the mean.

10. Interpreting the Magnitude of z

The absolute value tells us the distance from the mean.

Examples:

\[ z=+0.5 \]

means half a standard deviation above the mean.

\[ z=-1 \]

means one standard deviation below the mean.

\[ z=+2 \]

means two standard deviations above the mean.

The lecture explicitly emphasizes that a z score places the raw score into standard deviation units (Sigal, 2026).

11. Quick Example: Exam Score

Suppose:

\[ M=65,\quad SD=10,\quad X=83. \]

Then:

\[ z = \frac{83-65}{10} = 1.8. \]

Interpretation:

The score is 1.8 standard deviations above the mean.

12. Which z Score Is Closest to the Mean?

Suppose the options are:

  • (z=+0.50)
  • (z=+1.00)
  • (z=-1.00)
  • (z=-2.00)
  • (z=-0.25)

The score closest to the mean is:

\[ z=-0.25. \]

Why?

Because the mean corresponds to:

\[ z=0, \]

and (-0.25) has the smallest absolute distance from 0.

13. Standard Scores vs. z Scores

The Week 4 worksheet explicitly asks whether standardized scores and z scores are the same.

They are related, but not identical.

A standard score is the broader category.

A z score is one particular standardized scale with:

\[ M=0,\quad SD=1. \]

Other standardized scales can use different means and standard deviations.

Examples from the lecture include:

  • IQ-like scale: (M=100, SD=15)
  • T-score scale: (M=50, SD=10)
  • SAT-like scale: (M=500, SD=100)

(Sigal, 2026).

Part IV — The Normal Distribution

14. What Is a Normal Distribution?

The lecture describes the normal distribution as a theoretical curve that is often approximated by real data (Sigal, 2026).

It is defined by two parameters:

  • a mean;
  • a standard deviation.

The mean determines the center.

The standard deviation determines the width.

15. Properties of the Normal Distribution

The lecture emphasizes four practical properties:

  1. bell-shaped and single-peaked;
  2. symmetrical about the mean;
  3. 100% of scores fall under the curve;
  4. asymptotic tails extend indefinitely (Sigal, 2026).

The 2018 textbook provides a more detailed eight-property version, including theoretical definition, equal mean/median/mode, total area of 1, and asymptotic tails (Privitera, 2018).

16. From Histogram to Normal Curve

The lecture uses a sequence of height histograms to show how an empirical distribution can increasingly resemble a smooth bell-shaped curve as:

  • more observations are collected;
  • bins become narrower;
  • the distribution is viewed as a density rather than a few coarse frequency blocks.

The visual progression on slides 22–24 is important because it shows that a normal curve is a model of a distribution, not simply a decorative line drawn on top of data (Sigal, 2026).

17. Normal Does Not Mean Exact

The lecture explicitly treats the normal distribution as theoretical.

Real data may approximate the curve rather than match it perfectly.

The 2018 textbook makes the same point: behavioral data rarely fit the mathematical normal distribution exactly; the normal model is useful when the data are approximately normal (Privitera, 2018).

Part V — The Standard Normal Distribution

18. What Is the Standard Normal Distribution?

If every score in a normal distribution is converted to z scores, the result is the standard normal distribution.

It always has:

\[ \mu=0 \]

and:

\[ \sigma=1. \]

This is also called:

  • the z distribution;
  • the standard normal curve.

(Sigal, 2026; Privitera, 2018)

19. The 3-Sigma Rule

The lecture uses approximate proportions:

  • about 34% between the mean and (+1SD);
  • about 14% between (+1SD) and (+2SD);
  • about 2% beyond (+2SD).

By symmetry, the same applies on the negative side.

This gives the familiar empirical-rule approximation:

  • about 68% within (1SD);
  • about 95% within (2SD);
  • about 99.7% within (3SD).

20. Area Under the Curve as Probability

The lecture makes the graphical interpretation explicit:

Probability is represented by area under the normal curve (Sigal, 2026).

Thus:

  • larger area = larger probability;
  • smaller tail area = rarer score;
  • total area under the curve = 1.

This links distribution geometry directly to probability.

Part VI — The z Table

21. What the z Table Tells Us

The z table provides proportions associated with z scores.

The lecture uses a table with:

  • Area between Mean and z
  • Area beyond z

(see Lecture 4, slide 31).

The 2018 text labels these as:

  • Column B: area between the mean and z;
  • Column C: area beyond z toward the tail (Privitera, 2018).

22. Why Only Positive z Values?

The worksheet asks why the standard normal table includes only positive values.

The answer:

Because the standard normal distribution is symmetrical.

The area associated with:

\[ z=+1.2 \]

mirrors the area associated with:

\[ z=-1.2. \]

Listing both would be redundant (Sigal, 2026).

23. Can a Proportion Be Negative?

No.

A probability or proportion must satisfy:

\[ 0\le p\le1. \]

The smallest possible proportion is:

\[ 0. \]

The worksheet answer key explicitly reinforces this point (Sigal, 2026).

24. A Graphical Strategy

The lecture strongly recommends:

Always draw a diagram for z-score probability problems (Sigal, 2026).

A useful workflow is:

  1. draw the normal curve;
  2. mark the mean;
  3. locate the relevant z score;
  4. shade the requested area;
  5. decide whether the problem asks for:
    • area between mean and z;
    • tail area;
    • area below z;
    • area above z;
    • area between two z scores.

This visual step helps prevent table-lookup mistakes.

Part VII — Probability Problems With z Scores

25. Basic Procedure

To find the probability associated with a raw score:

Step 1

Convert the raw score to z.

Step 2

Look up the z score in the z table.

Step 3

Use the appropriate area column based on the diagram.

(Sigal, 2026)

26. Example: Proportion Below a Score

Suppose:

\[ \mu=40,\quad \sigma=10,\quad X=35. \]

Then:

\[ z = \frac{35-40}{10} = -0.5. \]

The area between the mean and (z=0.50) is approximately:

\[ 0.1915. \]

Therefore the area below:

\[ z=-0.50 \]

is:

\[ 0.5000-0.1915 = 0.3085. \]

So the proportion below (X=35) is:

\[ 0.3085. \]

This is the correct answer to the lecture concept check (Sigal, 2026).

27. Example: Between Two Scores

Suppose:

\[ M=195,\quad SD=30, \]

and we want the percentage between:

\[ X=165 \]

and:

\[ X=225. \]

Convert:

\[ z_{165} = \frac{165-195}{30} = -1, \]

and:

\[ z_{225} = \frac{225-195}{30} = +1. \]

The area between (z=-1) and (z=+1) is approximately:

\[ 0.6826 \]

or about:

\[ 68\%. \]

28. Example: Below the Mean

For any normal distribution:

\[ p(X<M)=0.50. \]

The mean divides the normal curve into two equal halves.

29. Example: Below (+1SD)

If:

\[ z=+1, \]

then the cumulative area below it is approximately:

\[ 0.8413. \]

So about:

\[ 84\% \]

of scores lie below (+1SD).

This matches the Week 4 worksheet answer key (Sigal, 2026).

Part VIII — Percentiles and Reverse Lookup

30. From Percentile to z Score

The lecture notes that the z table can be used in reverse.

If we know a percentile or tail probability, we can find the z score that corresponds to it.

Example:

What z score has 17% of scores above it?

Look for approximately:

\[ 0.17 \]

in the tail area column.

The corresponding z score is about:

\[ z\approx0.95. \]

(Sigal, 2026)

31. From z Score Back to a Raw Score

Starting with:

\[ z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}, \]

solve for (X):

\[ X=\mu+z\sigma. \]

This allows us to find a raw-score cutoff for a known percentile or probability.

32. Worksheet Algebra Example

The worksheet includes a rearrangement problem using:

\[ z=2.42. \]

The answer key solves:

\[ 2.42 = \frac{x-100}{15/\sqrt{25}}. \]

Because:

\[ \frac{15}{\sqrt{25}} = \frac{15}{5} = 3, \]

we get:

\[ 2.42 = \frac{x-100}{3}. \]

Thus:

\[ 7.26=x-100, \]

and:

\[ x=107.26. \]

This problem reinforces algebraic manipulation of standardized-score formulas (Sigal, 2026).

Part IX — Converting Between Metrics

33. Why Standardization Helps Comparison

Converting to z removes the original units.

The lecture gives the example of two variables with very different means and standard deviations. After conversion, both become:

\[ M=0,\quad SD=1. \]

This means scores on different measurement scales can be compared in terms of relative standing (Sigal, 2026).

34. Comparing Different Variables

The lecture gives examples such as:

  • social anxiety;
  • height;
  • emotional intelligence.

Suppose one person’s scores convert to:

  • social anxiety: (z=-0.5)
  • height: (z=0.3)
  • emotional intelligence: (z=2.0)

Then the interpretation is:

  • somewhat below average on social anxiety;
  • near average on height;
  • well above average on emotional intelligence.

The original units are different, but z scores place all three on a common relative scale.

35. Converting to a New Standardized Scale

Suppose a raw score has:

\[ z=+2. \]

We want to express that same relative position on a new scale with:

\[ M=100,\quad SD=50. \]

Then:

\[ X = 100+2(50) = 200. \]

So a z score of (+2) corresponds to a raw score of:

\[ 200 \]

on the new metric.

36. Real-World Standardized Scales

The lecture highlights several familiar standardized metrics:

  • IQ: (M=100, SD=15)
  • T scores: (M=50, SD=10)
  • SAT-like scores: (M=500, SD=100)

These are all examples of changing the scale while preserving relative position (Sigal, 2026).

Part X — Standardizing Does Not Normalize

37. A Crucial Distinction

One of the most important Week 4 ideas is:

Converting scores to z does not change the shape of the distribution (Sigal, 2026).

If the original distribution is positively skewed, the z-score distribution will still be positively skewed.

Standardization changes:

  • center;
  • scale.

It does not change:

  • rank order;
  • relative spacing;
  • distribution shape.

38. Worked Skewed Example

The lecture uses:

\[ X= \{10,10,10,10,10,10,10,10,11,11,11,14,14,16,19\}. \]

The distribution is positively skewed.

The lecture gives:

\[ \bar{x}=11.73 \]

and:

\[ s=2.76. \]

After converting all raw scores to z scores, the new distribution has:

\[ \bar{z}=0 \]

and:

\[ s_z=1. \]

But the histogram remains positively skewed.

The lecture explicitly states:

This data has not been “normalized” (Sigal, 2026).

If the worksheet scores are standardized, the resulting z-score distribution has:

\[ M=0 \]

and:

But its positive skew remains.

  • standardization;

41. Store-Count Example

The worksheet asks about counts of people entering a store.

Assume:

\[ M=975,\quad SD=15. \]

Between 960 and 990

These values are:

\[ z=-1 \]

and:

\[ z=+1. \]

So approximately:

\[ 68\% \]

of counts lie between them.

Below 975

Because 975 is the mean:

\[ 50\% \]

lie below it.

Below 990

Because:

\[ 990=975+15, \]

we have:

\[ z=+1. \]

Thus approximately:

\[ 84\% \]

lie below 990.

These are the worksheet-key answers (Sigal, 2026).

Part XII — Common Misunderstandings

42. Common Errors With z Scores

“A negative z score means a bad score.”

No. It only means the score lies below the mean.

“A larger raw score always means better relative performance.”

No. Relative standing depends on the mean and standard deviation.

“A z score is the only type of standard score.”

No. It is one specific standardized scale.

“Standardizing makes a distribution normal.”

No. It preserves distribution shape.

“The z table only applies to positive raw scores.”

No. It is based on symmetry around (z=0).

“A negative z score implies a negative probability.”

No. Probabilities remain between 0 and 1.

“If z = 0, the raw score must be 0.”

No. (z=0) means the raw score equals the mean.

Part XIII — Tutorial Preparation

43. Questions a TA Should Be Ready to Answer

“Why bother converting to z?”

Because raw scores are hard to interpret without context. z expresses a score relative to its distribution.

“Why divide by SD?”

Because the standard deviation defines the distribution’s unit of spread. Dividing by SD expresses distance from the mean in common units.

“Why is the mean of z always 0?”

Because standardization subtracts the original mean from every score.

“Why is the SD of z always 1?”

Because the deviations are divided by the original standard deviation.

“Why can we use only positive values in the table?”

Because the normal distribution is symmetric.

“Why should I draw the curve?”

Because most z-table mistakes come from choosing the wrong area.

“Why does a skewed distribution stay skewed after standardization?”

Because standardization is a linear transformation.

Suppose:

\[ M=50,\quad SD=12. \]

47. Normal-Curve Areas

Without using a table first, decide whether each area should be:

  • less than .50;
  • equal to .50;
  • greater than .50.
  1. (p(z>1))
  2. (p(z<0))
  3. (p(z<1))
  4. (p(-1<z<1))

Then verify using the table.

48. Standardization and Shape

Suppose a distribution is strongly positively skewed.

After converting every score to z:

  1. what is the new mean?
  2. what is the new SD?
  3. what happens to the skew?
  4. has the distribution been normalized?

Part XVIII — Week 4 Takeaway

53. Week 4 Takeaway

The central idea of Week 4 is relative position.

A raw score tells us what was observed.

A z score tells us:

Where that observation sits relative to the rest of its distribution.

The core formula is:

\[ z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}. \]

From there:

  • the sign tells us direction;
  • the magnitude tells us distance;
  • the standard normal distribution gives us a common scale;
  • the z table converts distance into probability.

Most importantly:

Standardizing a distribution changes its location and scale, but not its shape.

That distinction becomes essential as the course moves into probability, sampling distributions, and inferential statistics.
