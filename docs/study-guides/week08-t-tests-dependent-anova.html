Week 08 — Dependent-Samples t-tests and ANOVA

Overview

Week 8 extends the reasoning of Weeks 5–7 in two directions. In Week 7, two independent groups supplied two estimates of population means. But sometimes the scores are linked: a person is measured before and after an intervention, or participants are deliberately matched. In that situation, treating the two columns as independent throws away important information. What matters is the change within each pair.

A second problem arises when a researcher wants to compare three or more means. A single subtraction cannot summarize all possible differences, and running many separate t tests increases the risk of false-positive results. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) gives us a way to test the overall pattern of means by comparing different sources of variability.

The intellectual sequence is:

Related observations → Difference scores → Paired-samples t test → Design, uncertainty and power → Multiple comparisons → Between- and within-group variance → F ratio → Post hoc comparisons and extensions.

The main guide follows Sigal (2026), Lecture 8, Repeated t and Beyond!, including the annotated slides. Privitera (2018, Chapters 10–12) supplies the supporting concepts and examples, with the 2024 chapters providing further explanations. The lecture’s final reading slide (41) specifies Chapter 10, excluding proportion of variance, and Sections 11.1–11.2 of Chapter 11. Detailed ANOVA computations and factorial designs below are explicitly marked as extensions; do not interpret them as additional confirmed examination requirements. The slide’s assigned problem page numbers refer to the course’s pagination, which differs from the supplied PDF excerpts.

Learning Objectives

By the end of Week 8, you should be able to:

  1. Select an appropriate z test, one-sample t test, independent-samples t test or paired-samples t test based on the research design.
  2. Distinguish independent samples, repeated measures, pre-post designs and naturally or deliberately matched pairs.
  3. Compute signed difference scores, their mean, sum of squares, sample variance, SD and estimated SE.
  4. State hypotheses about the population mean difference and explain its sampling distribution.
  5. Calculate degrees of freedom, locate critical t values, make a one- or two-tailed decision and interpret a p value.
  6. Construct and interpret a paired confidence interval, and relate it to the corresponding two-tailed test.
  7. Calculate paired Cohen’s d when required by course convention and distinguish it from the t statistic.
  8. Evaluate paired-test assumptions, visualize difference scores, explain the advantages and drawbacks of pairing, and report results appropriately.
  9. Explain why multiple unadjusted tests inflate family-wise error, and calculate the number of pairwise comparisons and illustrative error rates.
  10. Identify factors, levels, group means and the weighted grand mean; explain the null and alternative hypotheses for ANOVA.
  11. Partition total variability into between- and within-group sums of squares, calculate mean squares and an F ratio, and read an F table.
  12. Interpret an omnibus ANOVA, understand the purpose of post hoc tests, and distinguish one-way, repeated-measures, factorial and mixed designs.
  13. As textbook extensions, interpret the removal of between-person variability, sphericity, main effects, interactions and simple effects.

Part I — From Independent Samples to Related Samples

1. Revisiting the tests we already know

Lecture 8 (slides 3–4) starts by recalling the progression from z to t. The one-sample z test requires a fixed reference mean and known population SD. When the SD is unknown, a one-sample t test estimates it from the sample. With two independent groups, the reference becomes the difference between population means, usually zero under the null hypothesis. The pooled independent-samples test from Week 7 is:

\[ s_p^2=\frac{SS_1+SS_2}{(n_1-1)+(n_2-1)},\qquad t=\frac{\bar X_1-\bar X_2}{\sqrt{s_p^2/n_1+s_p^2/n_2}}. \]

The pooled version assumes equal population variances. Welch’s test is an alternative for independent groups when the common-variance assumption is not appropriate. Neither independent test accounts for repeated observations from the same individual.

Research design What is compared? Appropriate procedure
One sample, fixed mean and known population SD Mean vs. benchmark One-sample z test
One sample, fixed mean, unknown population SD Mean vs. benchmark One-sample t test
Two unrelated groups Difference between population means Independent-samples t test
Two related conditions or matched pairs Mean of paired differences Paired-samples t test
Three or more independent groups, one factor Pattern of population means One-way between-subjects ANOVA
Three or more conditions, same people Pattern of condition means One-way repeated-measures ANOVA

2. Recognizing a dependent-samples design

The lecture uses the terms repeated measures, correlated measures, before-and-after, matched design and pre-test/post-test (slide 4). They share a statistical feature: each observation in one condition has an identifiable partner in the other. Privitera (2018, Section 10.1) distinguishes how the pairing is created:

  • A pre-post design measures the same person before and after a treatment.
  • A within-subjects design exposes the same person to different conditions, which need not have a fixed before-after order.
  • A matched-pairs design uses different but deliberately paired participants, for instance two people with similar baseline scores, or naturally matched participants such as twins.

In matched pairs, one difference represents a pair, not an individual. In repeated measures, one difference represents the change for one participant. Two groups that happen to have the same sample size are not necessarily paired. Pairing must come from the study design and identifiable links in the data.

3. Change as a difference score

The before-and-after dog illustration on slides 5–7 motivates a simple operation: subtract the earlier measurement from the later measurement. Define the direction before calculating:

\[ D_i=X_{\mathrm{post},i}-X_{\mathrm{pre},i},\qquad \bar D=\frac{\sum D_i}{n}. \]

A positive difference means that the measured outcome increased. Whether that increase is an improvement depends on the outcome: higher satisfaction may be desirable, but higher anxiety or greater distance from a target may not be. Negative differences must be retained rather than converted to absolute values. Reversing the order for every pair changes the sign of the mean, t and d but not the two-tailed p value; the CI changes sign and reverses its endpoints.

ImportantThe unit of analysis

Eight participants measured twice provide 16 raw scores but only eight independent paired differences. The paired t test analyzes those eight differences. Keep participant IDs aligned across conditions and never sort each column separately.

4. Which sampling distribution?

The handwritten diagram on annotated slide 8 imagines repeatedly drawing sets of paired differences and computing their means. The sampling distribution of \(\bar D\) is centered at the population mean difference \(\mu_D\). Under the usual null hypothesis, this center is zero. (The slides write the population mean difference with a bar over D in the subscript; the textbook writes \(\mu_D\).)

If the population SD of differences were known, the standard error would be \(\sigma_D/\sqrt n\). Normally it is unknown, so we estimate it with \(s_D/\sqrt n\). Thus the related-samples t test is mathematically a one-sample t test applied to a new variable, the difference score, with reference mean zero.

Distinguish the distribution of individual differences from the sampling distribution of their means. The first describes how individual changes vary; the second describes uncertainty in the average change. Under independent normally distributed differences, the standardized test statistic follows Student’s t with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom under the null.

Part II — The Paired-Samples t Test: Calculation and Interpretation

5. Four steps of hypothesis testing

Step 1 — State hypotheses. For a nondirectional question about change:

\[ H_0:\mu_D=0,\qquad H_1:\mu_D\ne0. \]

A directional alternative may instead specify \(\mu_D>0\) or \(\mu_D<0\). Choose the direction before examining the results; for an outcome such as errors, a decrease may be the predicted improvement. The null boundary for the directional test is zero.

Step 2 — Set the decision criterion. Choose \(\alpha\) and one or two tails. Find \(df=n-1\) (number of complete pairs minus one). For a two-tailed .05 test, find the positive critical value corresponding to .025 in one tail, and use both positive and negative boundaries. For a directional test, place all alpha in the specified tail.

df One-tail .05 Two-tail .05 Two-tail .01
3 2.353 3.182 5.841
4 2.132 2.776 4.604
5 2.015 2.571 4.032
7 1.895 2.365 3.499
9 1.833 2.262 3.250
14 1.761 2.145 2.977

Step 3 — Compute the statistic. Use the differences, not the raw pre/post columns:

\[ SS_D=\sum(D_i-\bar D)^2=\sum D_i^2-\frac{(\sum D_i)^2}{n}, \]

\[ s_D^2=\frac{SS_D}{n-1},\qquad s_D=\sqrt{s_D^2},\qquad s_{\bar D}=\frac{s_D}{\sqrt n}. \]

\[ t_{obt}=\frac{\bar D-\mu_{D,0}}{s_{\bar D}}. \]

Step 4 — Decide and interpret. For a two-tailed test, reject if the absolute obtained t exceeds the critical magnitude, or equivalently if \(p<\alpha\). Otherwise fail to reject. The p value is a tail probability under the null, not the probability that the null is true. Write the conclusion about average change rather than claiming that every participant changed or that no effect exists.

Step 1. \(H_0:\mu_D=0\) versus \(H_1:\mu_D\ne0\).

Step 2. With \(n=8\), \(df=7\), and two-tailed \(\alpha=.05\), \(t_{crit}=\pm2.365\).

Step 3. The sum of the differences is 47 and the sum of their squares is 1395. Therefore:

\[ \bar D=47/8=5.875,\qquad SS_D=1395-47^2/8=1118.875. \]

\[ s_D=\sqrt{1118.875/7}=12.64276,\qquad s_{\bar D}=12.64276/\sqrt8=4.46989. \]

\[ t(7)=\frac{5.875}{4.46989}=1.31435. \]

Step 4. The statistic lies inside the nonrejection region because \(|1.31435|<2.365\). The exact two-tailed p value is .230. Fail to reject the null: the sample shows an increase of 5.875 points, but these data do not provide sufficient evidence of a population mean change at .05.

Slide 17 reports 1.32 using rounded intermediate values; slide 22 reports 1.314. Both lead to the same decision. Carry full precision to the final step.

7. Confidence intervals and effect size

The paired confidence interval estimates the population mean change, using the same estimated SE as the test (slide 18):

\[ CI_{1-\alpha}=\bar D\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-1}s_{\bar D}. \]

For employee satisfaction, the 95% CI is \(5.875\pm2.364624\times4.469889=[-4.69,16.44]\) points (the slide gives [-4.69, 16.45] because of rounding). Zero is inside the interval, so the corresponding two-sided test does not reject at .05. The interval also includes potentially meaningful improvements. It does not establish equality or describe the range of individual changes. A 95% confidence procedure produces intervals covering the fixed population mean in 95% of repeated samples under its assumptions.

Cohen’s d for paired differences:

\[ d=\frac{\bar D}{s_D}=\frac{t}{\sqrt n}\quad(\mu_{D,0}=0). \]

This version is also called \(d_z\). It standardizes the change by the SD of individual changes, not by their SE or by pooled raw-score SD. For a significant result, interpret the sign and magnitude; conventional reference points are .20, .50 and .80 for small, medium and large effects. They are guides, not universal thresholds of practical importance.

NoteCourse convention and research practice

Slide 19 asks students to compute effect size only after rejecting the null hypothesis. Follow this convention for course questions unless asked otherwise. The lecture nevertheless illustrates the nonsignificant satisfaction example: \(d=5.875/12.64276=0.465\), near the medium reference point. In general research practice, an effect estimate and its uncertainty are informative even if a test is nonsignificant. The lecture excludes the textbook’s paired proportion-of-variance measures from the required reading, so they are not required calculations here.

8. Technical profile and visual diagnostics

The paired test requires meaningful quantitative difference scores (typically interval or ratio), genuine matching and independence between pairs, not within a pair. For exact small-sample t inference, the population of differences should be approximately normal; larger samples are often more robust, but severe outliers or heavy skew may still matter (Privitera, 2018, Sections 10.2–10.3; 2024, Chapter 11).

Check the difference scores, rather than demanding that each raw condition be normal or have equal variance. A boxplot shows the median, middle half of the scores, spread and possible outliers. A Q–Q plot compares observed quantiles with a normal distribution; substantial bends or extreme points suggest departures. With eight pairs, neither a visual check nor a nonsignificant normality test proves normality.

The lecture’s slide 21 boxplot shows that changes are not uniformly positive. The median difference is 4, while the mean is 5.875. A zero reference line helps identify declines and improvements. Do not remove a point simply because it is inconvenient; check data quality and the study design first.

9. Reporting the paired test

An APA-style report, using the full-precision calculations from the lecture data, would read:

Employee satisfaction averaged 87.88 points before and 93.75 points after the agreement. A paired-samples t test did not detect a statistically significant mean change, t(7) = 1.31, p = .230, mean change = 5.88 points, 95% CI [-4.69, 16.44].

If requested, report the condition SDs or the SD of the differences clearly labelled. Use p < .001 rather than p = .000. Do not interpret the mean change as a causal effect unless the design rules out plausible alternative explanations such as other organizational changes.

10. Revisiting the data — removing administration

Slides 23–24 identify the first two employees as administration and repeat the calculations without them. The remaining differences are 8, 0, 2, 19, 28 and 6. The mean before is 84.00 and the mean after is 94.50.

\[ \bar D=63/6=10.5,\quad SS_D=587.5,\quad df=5. \]

\[ s_D=\sqrt{587.5/5}=10.83974,\quad s_{\bar D}=4.42531,\quad t(5)=2.37272. \]

The two-tailed .05 critical value is now 2.571, not 2.365, because there are fewer pairs. The two-tailed p value is .064 and 95% CI [-0.88, 21.88]. Fail to reject again, although the mean difference is larger. The lecture’s 2.38 is a rounded calculation.

This is an instructive sensitivity analysis: the first two negative changes affected both the mean and variability. It is not a license to delete observations until the result becomes significant. A subgroup analysis should have a substantive justification, ideally specified before looking at results, and the full-sample result should remain visible. A formal comparison of administration and other employees’ changes would be a different question.

Part III — Design, Power, and the Transition to ANOVA

11. Why pairing can increase power

The annotated example on slide 25 compares scores 10, 20, 24, 36 with 14, 25, 26, 40. The raw scores vary greatly across people, yet the changes are only 4, 5, 2 and 4. A person’s stable baseline is present in both scores and cancels on subtraction. The difference scores are less variable, so the SE of their average can be much smaller.

A useful textbook-supported explanation is the variance identity:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(D)=\operatorname{Var}(X_{post})+\operatorname{Var}(X_{pre})-2\operatorname{Cov}(X_{post},X_{pre}). \]

Positive covariance often reduces the variation of the differences. Pairing is therefore frequently more powerful and can be more practical, especially in learning and development research. It is not guaranteed to improve power in every situation: poor matching, negative correlation, noisy change scores and missing measurements can reduce the advantage. The test must follow the design, not the desired significance.

12. Disadvantages and ways to improve design

Slide 26 identifies carryover effects, fatigue, annoyance, memory and the need for comparable test forms. A drug might remain active during a second condition; a memory task might be easier because participants remember the first test; performance might deteriorate from fatigue. Such changes can be confounded with the treatment.

When the research question allows it, counterbalance the order (some participants experience A then B, others B then A), use comparable forms of a test, or allow an appropriate interval between conditions. A fixed pre-post design cannot simply reverse time; a suitable comparison group or additional design controls may be needed. Participants missing one of their two measurements cannot supply a complete difference; report missingness and do not treat unpaired scores as matched.

14. Which test do I use?

The decision tree on lecture slide 27 starts with the number of levels of the independent variable and then the knowledge of the population or dependence of the samples. Read the design before choosing a formula.

  • One observed sample against a fixed benchmark: known population SD → z; unknown → one-sample t.
  • Two conditions: independent units → independent t; linked units → paired t.
  • More than two conditions: one factor, independent units → one-way between-subjects ANOVA; same units → repeated-measures ANOVA.
  • Two or more factors: factorial ANOVA, with the error term appropriate to the between/within structure.

The response should be quantitative for these classical mean-based methods. Counts of people in categories lead to different methods later in the course. ANOVA can also compare two means; for exactly two groups the classical matched model has \(F=t^2\) (pooled independent test for between subjects, paired test for within subjects).

15. Why not just perform multiple t tests?

Lecture slides 28–31 introduce two difficulties with three or more group means. There is no single signed subtraction representing every difference, and testing every pair inflates the chance of at least one Type I error. With \(k\) groups there are:

\[ m=\frac{k(k-1)}{2} \]

unique pairwise comparisons. For three groups, these are A–B, A–C and B–C. If all null hypotheses are true and the tests are independent, the probability of no false rejection is \((1-\alpha)^m\). The complement is the family-wise (experiment-wise) error rate:

\[ P(\text{at least one Type I error})=1-(1-\alpha)^m. \]

For three independent tests at .05, \(1-.95^3=.142625\), or 14.26%. The arithmetic is an illustration assuming independent tests; real pairwise comparisons sharing groups are generally dependent, so this expression need not be their exact family-wise rate.

Number of groups Pairwise tests Illustrative family-wise error at .05
2 1 5.0%
3 3 14.3%
4 6 26.5%
5 10 40.1%
6 15 53.7%

An omnibus ANOVA tests a single overall null at the chosen alpha. If it is significant, follow up using multiplicity-aware comparisons that address where the differences lie. Merely performing an omnibus test does not automatically justify an unlimited number of unadjusted tests.

Part IV — Analysis of Variance: From Group Means to the F Ratio

16. Factors, levels and hypotheses

A factor is an independent or quasi-independent variable defining groups or conditions. A level is one category or condition of that factor. One-way means one factor, not one group or one tail. For example, feedback type (positive, negative, none) is one factor with three levels; self-esteem is a quantitative dependent variable.

Use \(k\) for number of levels, \(n_j\) for the sample size in group \(j\), and \(N=\sum_j n_j\) for the total number of independent observations. The lecture sometimes uses lowercase n for this total (slide 36); this guide uses N to distinguish it from the per-group sample size, consistent with Privitera (2018, Sections 11.1–11.2).

\[ H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_k. \]

The alternative is that at least one population mean differs from another. It does not say all means differ. The textbook also expresses the null as zero variance among the population means, \(H_0:\sigma_\mu^2=0\), versus \(H_1:\sigma_\mu^2>0\). This is about variability of the means, not equality of the raw-score variances; equal population error variances are an assumption of classical ANOVA.

17. Between-group and within-group variation

Lecture slides 32–37 contrast the distance of group centers from the grand mean with the scatter of observations around each group’s own center. Imagine groups whose means differ by the same amount in two studies. When scores within groups vary little, the separation stands out. When within-group scores vary considerably, the separation is less compelling at the same sample size. This is the same signal-versus-noise reasoning used for t tests.

\[ \bar X_{GM}=\frac{\sum_j n_j\bar X_j}{N}. \]

Use a weighted grand mean for unequal group sizes; a simple average of group means works only for equally sized groups. The sums of squares partition the total variability:

\[ SS_T=\sum_j\sum_i(X_{ij}-\bar X_{GM})^2, \]

\[ SS_B=\sum_j n_j(\bar X_j-\bar X_{GM})^2,\qquad SS_W=\sum_j\sum_i(X_{ij}-\bar X_j)^2=\sum_j SS_j. \]

\[ SS_T=SS_B+SS_W. \]

The weight \(n_j\) is emphasized in the handwritten annotation to slide 34. Between-group SS captures differences among group centers; within-group SS captures scatter left after using the group means to describe observations. Error means unexplained variability under this model, not necessarily mistakes in data collection.

18. Degrees of freedom and mean squares

Recall that a sample variance is a sum of squared deviations divided by its degrees of freedom. The ANOVA divides each source of variability by the df appropriate to it:

\[ df_B=k-1,\qquad df_W=\sum_j(n_j-1)=N-k,\qquad df_T=N-1. \]

\[ MS_B=\frac{SS_B}{k-1},\qquad MS_W=\frac{SS_W}{N-k}. \]

\(MS_W\), also called \(MS_E\) or residual mean square, is the pooled within-group variance estimate. \(MS_B\) estimates the same error variance under the null; if true group means differ, it also reflects systematic group separation. Under the null both mean squares estimate the same variance, so the F ratio is typically around one (its exact expectation need not be one, particularly for small denominator df).

19. The F distribution and statistical decision

\[ F(df_B,df_W)=\frac{MS_B}{MS_W}. \]

F is a ratio of nonnegative quantities and cannot be negative. With nonzero error variance, if the sample group means are exactly the same, F is zero. Large ratios provide evidence against equal population means. The F distribution is right-skewed and indexed by two degrees of freedom; the rejection region for classical omnibus ANOVA is in the upper tail. A small F is not evidence of a difference in the other direction.

In the textbook’s F table, numerator df appear across columns and denominator df down rows; select the alpha level. A few useful critical values are:

Compare the obtained F to its critical value or use the upper-tail p value. Rejecting the omnibus null tells us that at least one mean differs; it does not identify which pairs differ or whether the difference matters practically.

A textbook counterexample: overlapping groups

Privitera (2018, Table 11.1, p. 568) gives a classroom-talking example: Psychology (5, 2, 4, 3, 1); Sociology (3, 2, 6, 5, 4); Biology (4, 6, 5, 4, 6). Their means are 3, 4 and 5, with grand mean 4. The group SS values are 10, 10 and 4, respectively. Thus \(SS_B=10\), \(SS_W=24\), \(MS_B=5\), \(MS_W=2\) and \(F(2,12)=2.50\), \(p=.124\). Fail to reject at .05. This contrast illustrates why visibly different sample means do not necessarily constitute evidence of population differences.

21. Assumptions, diagnostics and reporting

The classical between-subjects ANOVA assumes independent sampling units, a quantitative outcome, approximately normal errors within groups (especially important in small samples), and common population error variance. The textbook also emphasizes random sampling for generalization. Random assignment supports causal comparisons but does not by itself guarantee a representative sample. Inspect distributions or residual Q–Q plots, unusual scores, and group variances. A nonsignificant Levene test does not prove equal variances; with materially unequal variances, especially with unbalanced samples, a Welch ANOVA may be appropriate (supplementary method). Balanced group sizes are helpful but not a mathematical requirement of ordinary one-way ANOVA; the weighted SS formulas above work for unequal sizes.

For the worked recall example, an APA-style report is:

Mean recall scores differed across learning strategies, F(2, 9) = 24.00, p < .001. The means were 4.00, 6.00 and 8.00 for strategies A, B and C, respectively (SD = 0.82 in each group).

Report the group descriptives and sample sizes, the F statistic with both degrees of freedom, p value, and relevant follow-up comparisons. When asked, supplement with an effect-size estimate. In this one-way independent example, \(\eta^2=SS_B/SS_T=32/38=.842\) (textbook extension, Section 11.3); it describes the observed fraction of total SS associated with group membership, not the fraction of each person’s score caused by a treatment. Do not apply this formula uncritically to other designs; partial and generalized measures use different denominators.

22. Post hoc comparisons

Slide 39 gives three aims: compare means, avoid inflation of Type I error and maintain power. Different procedures balance these goals for different comparison families. The lecture names Bonferroni, Duncan’s multiple range test (MRT), Fisher’s LSD, Newman–Keuls, Scheffé, Tukey’s HSD, Dunnett’s correction and Benjamini–Hochberg.

Procedure What it is useful for Caution
Bonferroni A specified family of tests; use alpha/m or adjust p by m Conservative for large families, but does not require independence
Tukey HSD All pairwise mean comparisons after classical between-subjects ANOVA Standard formula assumes equal n; Tukey–Kramer handles unequal n

Worked extension — Tukey HSD. For equal group sizes, Privitera (2018, Section 11.4) uses a studentized-range critical value q, the residual mean square, and n per group:

\[ HSD=q_{1-\alpha;k,df_W}\sqrt{MS_W/n}. \]

For the recall data, \(q=3.94849\) for three groups, 9 error df and .05. Then \(HSD=3.94849\sqrt{(2/3)/4}=1.612\). Pairwise differences are 2 (B–A), 4 (C–A) and 2 (C–B); all exceed the threshold. Therefore each of these differences is significant with Tukey’s adjustment. For three prespecified comparisons, a Bonferroni procedure instead uses .05/3 = .01667 per test. The adjusted p values or intervals should be reported when making pair-specific claims.

23. Beyond F — other ANOVA designs

Lecture slide 40 introduces two-way, three-way, repeated-measures and mixed designs as extensions of the same variance-ratio reasoning. The following sections are textbook enrichment, not a statement of additional exam scope.

One-way within-subjects ANOVA

One factor has several levels and each person is observed at all levels. Stable between-person differences can be separated from the residual error, analogous to the paired t test. In a complete design with \(n\) people and \(k\) conditions, there are \(nk\) measurements but \(n\) independent people. Using condition means \(\bar X_{\cdot j}\) and person means \(\bar X_{i\cdot}\):

\[ SS_B=n\sum_j(\bar X_{\cdot j}-\bar X_{GM})^2,\qquad SS_P=k\sum_i(\bar X_{i\cdot}-\bar X_{GM})^2, \]

\[ SS_E=SS_T-SS_B-SS_P,\quad df_B=k-1,\quad df_P=n-1,\quad df_E=(k-1)(n-1). \]

The total df is \(nk-1\); the condition F ratio is \((SS_B/df_B)/(SS_E/df_E)\). Here the error term is the remaining person-by-condition variability, not all the raw within-condition SS. Sphericity requires equal population variances of all pairwise condition differences; it is automatic for two levels. With more levels, violations can make uncorrected inference unreliable; Greenhouse–Geisser or Huynh–Feldt df adjustments are textbook extensions. Use paired comparisons with multiplicity adjustment rather than ordinary independent-group Tukey tests for follow-up.

Two-way between-subjects ANOVA

A factorial design crosses levels of two factors. The annotation on slide 40 sketches a \(3\times2\) layout: three levels of one factor crossed with two of another make six cells. Privitera (2018, Sections 12.1–12.3) explains why this is useful: a hypothesis may depend on two variables or a second variable may help account for an alternative explanation. A full factorial has observations in every combination.

A cell mean summarizes a particular combination; a marginal mean averages across the other factor. A main effect is the difference across the levels of one factor, averaged over the other. An interaction is a change in the effect of one factor depending on the level of the other. Nonparallel lines suggest a sample interaction; crossing is not necessary and a graph alone cannot establish significance.

Worked textbook extension — sugar exposure and buffet presence. Privitera (2018, Example 12.1, Table 12.7, p. 660) gives times to complete a computer task in a balanced 3 × 2 design (6 people per cell). Higher times indicate slower performance. The cell means are:

The main effect of exposure summarizes its average pattern; the interaction concerns differences between those simple effects. This is why a marginal mean can hide an important pattern.

For a balanced between-subjects design with \(a\) levels of A, \(b\) of B and \(r\) participants per cell, \(N=abr\). The sums of squares are:

\[ SS_A=br\sum_i(\bar X_{i\cdot}-\bar X_{GM})^2,\quad SS_B=ar\sum_j(\bar X_{\cdot j}-\bar X_{GM})^2, \]

\[ SS_{AB}=r\sum_{i,j}(\bar X_{ij}-\bar X_{i\cdot}-\bar X_{\cdot j}+\bar X_{GM})^2. \]

The residual SS adds squared deviations around each cell mean. In this textbook example, independently recomputing from the six cells’ raw scores gives the following table; the three F tests share the residual MS.

A significant interaction is not the same as two significant main effects. Because exposure may be preexisting rather than randomly assigned, the result is not by itself proof of a causal exposure effect.

Other factorial designs

Two-way means two factors regardless of the number of levels. Three-way means three factors, potentially including two-way and three-way interactions. A mixed factorial design combines at least one between-subjects factor with a within-subjects factor; for instance, treatment group × time. A two-within design repeatedly observes each participant at every combination. The 2024 textbook (Chapter 14, Section 14.2) distinguishes these three factorial arrangements. The between-subjects F table above cannot simply be reused for mixed or repeated factorial data, which need the correct error term and covariance assumptions.

Worksheet 1 — Repeated vs. independent designs

Repeated measures observe the same individuals in two conditions, or more generally a related-samples design uses matched pairs. Independent samples contain separate unlinked observations. The first calls for differences within pairs; the second uses differences between independently estimated group means. Independence is still required between the paired units.

Worksheet 2 — Null hypothesis

In words: the population mean of the paired differences is zero (no average change). In symbols, \(H_0:\mu_D=0\). For a nondirectional alternative, \(H_1:\mu_D\ne0\). This does not claim that every person’s difference is zero.

False as a statement of the inferential target. ANOVA compares mean squares to test whether population treatment means differ; it is not a test of whether the raw-score variances of groups are equal. The latter is a separate assumption-related question (e.g., Levene’s test).

Worksheet 6 — How does F respond to changes?

Holding df, sample sizes and other components fixed, larger between-treatment differences increase \(MS_B\) and F. Greater variability inside treatments increases \(MS_W\) and decreases F. The ratio measures relative rather than absolute variability.

Worksheet 7 — Partitioning total variability

For the one-way independent-groups ANOVA, \(SS_T=SS_B+SS_W\). Between-group SS captures weighted deviations of group means from the grand mean; within-group SS captures deviations of individual scores from their own means. Divide each by its df, then calculate \(F=MS_B/MS_W\). The corresponding degrees of freedom also add: \(N-1=(k-1)+(N-k)\).

Common mistakes include counting two scores as two independent people; removing negative differences; using pooled raw SD in the paired test; treating a nonsignificant result as proof of no effect; comparing SS directly without dividing by df; using an unweighted grand mean in an unequal-size design; rejecting in the lower F tail; claiming all groups differ after a significant ANOVA; and assuming that crossing lines automatically establishes a significant interaction. Question 1 — Choosing a paired test (Multiple choice)

A researcher measures the same 18 students’ stress before and after a study-skills course. Which test compares their average scores?

  • A. One-sample z test
  • B. Paired-samples t test
  • C. Pooled independent-samples t test
  • D. One-way independent-groups ANOVA
TipAnswer

B. Paired-samples t test

NoteExplanation

The same students supply both measurements, so observations are linked within each student. Form 18 difference scores and test their mean against zero. The independent-samples test would ignore the connection and use an incorrect error model.

Question 2 — Matching different participants (Multiple choice)

Which study uses related samples even though each person is measured only once?

  • A. Two groups recruited independently from separate universities
  • B. A random assignment of 40 people to two groups without matching
  • C. One person from each of 15 matched pairs assigned to each condition
  • D. A sample of 30 people compared with a fixed national mean
TipAnswer

C. One person from each of 15 matched pairs assigned to each condition

NoteExplanation

Matching creates a defined relationship between the two observations in each pair. Even when the individuals differ, subtract the two observations within each pair, producing 15 independent differences if pairs are independent. Equal sample sizes alone do not establish matching.

Question 3 — Direction of difference (Multiple choice)

A participant completes a task in 42 seconds before practice and 35 seconds afterward. Define D = post − pre. What is D?

  • A. −7 seconds
  • B. +7 seconds
  • C. 35 seconds
  • D. 77 seconds
TipAnswer

A. −7 seconds

NoteExplanation

Subtract the original time from the later time: 35 − 42 = −7. The negative sign means that completion time decreased. Since faster completion may be desirable, a negative difference can indicate improvement.

Question 4 — Degrees of freedom (Multiple choice)

Twelve participants provide complete pre-post pairs. What is the df for the paired t test?

  • A. 23
  • B. 22
  • C. 12
  • D. 11
TipAnswer

D. 11

NoteExplanation

Each participant contributes a single difference, so n = 12, not 24. Estimating the mean of those differences uses one degree of freedom; df = n − 1 = 11.

Question 5 — Null hypothesis (Multiple choice)

For a nondirectional paired test of anxiety before versus after training, which is the correct null hypothesis?

  • A. \(\bar D=0\)
  • B. \(\mu_D=0\)
  • C. \(\mu_D\ne0\)
  • D. \(s_D=0\)
TipAnswer

B. \(\mu_D=0\)

NoteExplanation

The null hypothesis is a statement about the population mean difference, not the observed sample mean or its standard deviation. Individual scores may change in either direction while the population average difference is zero. The alternative specifies a nonzero population mean change.

Question 6 — Estimated standard error (Multiple choice)

Twenty-five paired differences have \(s_D=10\). What is the estimated SE of the mean difference?

  • A. 0.40
  • B. 0.20
  • C. 2.00
  • D. 10.00
TipAnswer

C. 2.00

NoteExplanation

The SD of individual changes is 10, while the estimated SD of the sampling distribution of their mean is 10 divided by the square root of 25. The SE is 2. It decreases with the square root of the number of independent pairs, not directly with that number.

Question 7 — Critical t and statistical decision (Multiple choice)

For a two-tailed test at .05, eight pairs yield \(t=-2.10\). The critical values are ±2.365. What is the decision?

  • A. Fail to reject the null
  • B. Reject because t is negative
  • C. Reject because 2.10 exceeds 1.96
  • D. Reject because −2.10 is less than +2.365
TipAnswer

A. Fail to reject the null

NoteExplanation

The paired test uses the t distribution with seven degrees of freedom, not the standard normal distribution. Compare the magnitude of the observed t with 2.365: 2.10 is smaller. The result is not significant at .05, but this does not prove the population mean change is zero.

Question 8 — Confidence intervals (Multiple choice)

A paired 95% CI for post minus pre is [−1.2, 4.8]. What follows for the matching two-tailed test?

  • A. Reject at .05 because the upper limit is positive
  • B. Reject at .05 because the interval is wide
  • C. All individuals improved
  • D. Fail to reject at .05
TipAnswer

D. Fail to reject at .05

NoteExplanation

Zero is a possible population mean difference under this confidence procedure because the interval crosses zero. The matching two-sided .05 test therefore fails to reject. The interval estimates average change, so it cannot show that every individual changed in the same direction.

Question 9 — Cohen’s d (Multiple choice)

For a significant paired result, \(\bar D=6\) and \(s_D=8\), with \(n=16\). What is the paired Cohen’s d?

  • A. 3.00
  • B. 0.75
  • C. 0.375
  • D. 0.046875
TipAnswer

B. 0.75

NoteExplanation

Paired Cohen’s d divides the average change by the SD of differences: 6/8 = 0.75. Dividing by the SE, 8/4 = 2, instead gives t = 3. The effect-size statistic describes change relative to individual variability, not the precision of the estimated mean.

Question 10 — Assumptions (Multiple choice)

What is the most relevant distribution to inspect for a small-sample paired t test?

  • A. The distribution of participant IDs
  • B. The pooled pre and post scores
  • C. The distribution of paired differences
  • D. Only the distribution of the post-test scores
TipAnswer

C. The distribution of paired differences

NoteExplanation

The paired statistic is a one-sample test on difference scores. Thus the normality requirement for exact small-sample inference concerns the population of differences. The separate pre and post distributions may differ in spread, and that alone does not invalidate a paired test.

Question 11 — Order effects (Multiple choice)

Participants do the same memory test twice, first in silence and then with music. Which threat is especially relevant?

  • A. Practice or memory of the first test
  • B. Unequal numbers of independent groups
  • C. Incorrect population mean in a one-sample z test
  • D. The need to pool independent variances
TipAnswer

A. Practice or memory of the first test

NoteExplanation

The second test can be affected by what was learned in the first test. Because music always occurs second, improvement due to practice can be mistaken for an effect of music. Counterbalancing the order and using comparable test forms can help separate these influences.

Question 12 — Multiple comparisons (Multiple choice)

How many unique pairs can be compared among five groups?

  • A. 5
  • B. 15
  • C. 20
  • D. 10
TipAnswer

D. 10

NoteExplanation

There are five ways to choose a first group and four ways to choose a second, but each pair is counted twice. Dividing 5 × 4 by 2 gives 10. Running all ten unadjusted tests creates a family of comparisons, so the overall false-positive risk is larger than .05.

Question 13 — Family-wise error (Multiple choice)

Assume three independent tests, all null hypotheses true, and alpha = .05 for each. What is the probability of at least one Type I error?

  • A. 5.0%
  • B. 14.3%
  • C. 15.0%
  • D. 85.7%
TipAnswer

B. 14.3%

NoteExplanation

The probability of no false rejection across all three independent tests is .95 cubed, or .857375. The complement, .142625, is the probability of one or more false rejections. The independence assumption matters: comparisons sharing groups need not satisfy it.

Question 14 — ANOVA hypotheses (Multiple choice)

Which is the alternative hypothesis in a one-way ANOVA comparing four population means?

  • A. Every population mean differs from every other mean
  • B. All population variances are unequal
  • C. At least one population mean differs from another
  • D. The group means are equal but the sample sizes differ
TipAnswer

C. At least one population mean differs from another

NoteExplanation

The omnibus test asks whether all population means can be treated as equal. Evidence against equality implies at least one difference, not necessarily a difference in every pair. Equality of error variances is a model assumption rather than the hypothesis tested by the ANOVA F ratio.

Question 15 — Mean squares (Multiple choice)

An ANOVA has \(SS_B=30\), \(df_B=2\), \(SS_W=60\), and \(df_W=12\). What is F?

  • A. 3.00
  • B. 0.50
  • C. 2.00
  • D. 6.00

A. 3.00

NoteExplanation

Divide each SS by its corresponding df before forming the ratio: MS between = 15 and MS within = 5. Their ratio equals 3. Directly dividing the sums of squares gives 0.50, which does not account for the different degrees of freedom.

Question 16 — Interactions — textbook extension (Multiple choice)

In a factorial plot, two lines have different slopes but do not cross within the observed range. What can we infer from the plot alone?

  • A. There cannot be an interaction
  • B. Both main effects are necessarily significant

Nonparallel slopes mean the effect of one factor differs across levels of another in the observed cell means. The lines need not cross to show that pattern. Sampling variation may explain the pattern, so a formal interaction test is necessary to assess significance.

Question 17 — Difference scores from a small dataset

Five students take a practice quiz and then a second quiz. Their before scores are 12, 15, 10, 16, 14; their after scores are 15, 17, 9, 20, 16. Define D = after − before. Find all D values, their mean and df.

\(D=(3,2,-1,4,2)\), \(\sum D=10\), \(\bar D=2\), \(df=5-1=4\).

Question 18 — A complete paired t test

A researcher records six independent pairs of scores. Differences (after − before) are 2, 4, 3, 1, 5, 3. State hypotheses, compute the difference SD, SE, obtained t, df, and decision. Use critical values ±2.571.

\(H_0:\mu_D=0\); \(H_1:\mu_D\ne0\). \(\bar D=3\), \(SS_D=10\), \(s_D=\sqrt{10/5}=1.4142\), \(SE=1.4142/\sqrt6=.57735\), \(t(5)=5.196\). Reject \(H_0\); two-tailed \(p=.0035\).

NoteExplanation

The six differences constitute the entire sample for the test. Their deviations from 3 are −1, 1, 0, −2, 2 and 0, giving a squared total of 10. The mean change is over five estimated standard errors from zero, so it exceeds the two-tailed threshold of 2.571. There is evidence of a positive population mean change under the model assumptions.

Question 19 — CI and effect size for a paired test

Use the six difference scores in Question 18. Construct the 95% CI and calculate paired Cohen’s d.

\(95\%\ CI=3\pm2.570582(.57735)=[1.52,4.48]\). \(d=3/1.4142=2.12\).

Question 20 — Reversing the subtraction

Ten pairs yield a mean difference of +4, SD of differences 5, and t = 2.530 using post minus pre. If you redefine D as pre minus post, what happens to the mean, SD, SE, t, d and two-tailed p?

TipAnswer

New mean = −4; SD = 5; SE = \(5/\sqrt{10}=1.5811\); t = −2.530; d = −0.80; two-tailed p unchanged.

NoteExplanation

Multiplication by −1 reverses each difference and its mean but leaves the squared deviations unchanged. Therefore SD and SE are identical. Both t and d use the signed mean difference, so they change sign. A two-tailed test depends on the magnitude of t, although a directional interpretation must be reversed consistently.

Question 21 — Statistical power and individual differences

Two conditions have raw-score SDs of 10 each. In a paired design their population correlation is .80. Compute the population SD of the paired differences using the variance identity. Compare it with the SD of a difference of independent individual scores with the same marginal variances.

TipAnswer

Paired: \(\sigma_D=\sqrt{100+100-2(.80)(10)(10)}=\sqrt{40}=6.32\). Independent: \(\sqrt{100+100}=14.14\).

NoteExplanation

The covariance term is positive because people with high scores in one condition tend to have high scores in the other. Subtracting within a pair removes much of this shared baseline variation, leaving a smaller difference SD. With the same number of pairs, a smaller SD also means a smaller SE of the mean difference. This is why pairing often improves power, provided the pairing is meaningful.

Question 22 — Reporting a nonsignificant paired result

A paired experiment with 15 people has a mean change of 2 points, t(14) = 1.10 and two-tailed p = .290. A student concludes, “The treatment has no effect.” Rewrite the conclusion and explain what else should be reported.

TipAnswer

The observed mean change was 2 points, but the paired-samples test did not detect a statistically significant population mean change, t(14) = 1.10, p = .290. Report the 95% CI, descriptive statistics, and the sample size. Follow course instructions on whether to calculate d.

NoteExplanation

A p value above .05 means insufficient evidence to reject the null under the specified test. It does not demonstrate an exactly zero effect. The estimated mean change remains two points, and a confidence interval would indicate the range of changes compatible with the data. Study design and measurement quality also affect the interpretation.

Question 23 — Subgroup sensitivity

The full satisfaction dataset gives t(7) = 1.314. After removing the two administrators, the result becomes t(5) = 2.373. At .05 two-tailed, what are the decisions? Why is it inappropriate to keep removing participants until p is below .05?

TipAnswer

Full sample: \(1.314<2.365\), fail to reject. Restricted sample: \(2.373<2.571\), fail to reject. Removing observations solely to obtain significance creates a selective analysis and undermines the nominal Type I error control.

NoteExplanation

The subgroup has a larger mean change but fewer degrees of freedom, so its critical value also increases. A valid subgroup analysis needs a research justification, not simply a more favorable statistic. Reporting both estimates transparently shows how sample composition influences the conclusion. Neither test provides convincing evidence at the specified two-tailed threshold.

Question 24 — Family-wise error across six comparisons

Four independent treatment groups are compared pairwise. How many tests are possible? Under the illustrative assumption of independent true-null tests, calculate the family-wise error at .05 per test. What Bonferroni per-test threshold controls the family-wise rate at .05?

TipAnswer

\(m=4(3)/2=6\). \(1-.95^6=.26491\) (26.49%). Bonferroni threshold = \(.05/6=.00833\).

NoteExplanation

Six tests offer six opportunities for false rejection. Taking the complement of the probability of no errors gives 26.49% only under the stated independence and true-null conditions. Bonferroni controls the family-wise rate at no more than .05 by dividing the allowable error across the six tests; it does not rely on independence of those comparisons.

Question 25 — Weighted grand mean and between-group SS

Three independent groups have \(n_1=3\), \(n_2=5\), \(n_3=2\) and means 4, 6 and 9. Compute the grand mean, \(SS_B\) and \(MS_B\).

TipAnswer

\(N=10\), \(\bar X_{GM}=(3\times4+5\times6+2\times9)/10=6\). \(SS_B=3(4-6)^2+5(6-6)^2+2(9-6)^2=30\). \(df_B=2\); \(MS_B=15\).

NoteExplanation

Each group mean represents a different number of observations, so the grand mean must weight the means by their sample sizes. The squared distances of the group centers are likewise multiplied by those sample sizes. Dividing the resulting between-group SS by two degrees of freedom converts it to a mean square, which can be compared with the residual mean square.

The three groups each have within-group SS of 2, so their total residual SS is 6. After dividing by the respective df, the between mean square is twelve times the residual mean square. The omnibus test establishes evidence of a difference somewhere, not which groups differ.

Question 27 — Completing and interpreting an F test

For a study of four independent groups, N = 20, total SS = 100 and within SS = 64. Complete the ANOVA table and make a decision using the .05 critical value 3.239 for F(3,16).

TipAnswer

\(SS_B=100-64=36\); \(df_B=3\), \(df_W=16\), \(df_T=19\); \(MS_B=12\), \(MS_W=4\), \(F(3,16)=3.00\). Fail to reject at .05; p ≈ .062.

NoteExplanation

The total SS separates into variation explained by group centers and unexplained variation within groups. The between mean square is three times the residual mean square, but the F reference distribution is needed to determine whether that ratio is sufficiently unusual. Here 3.00 is below 3.239. Neither a ratio above one nor distinct sample means automatically implies a significant result.

Question 28 — Tukey comparisons (textbook extension)

Three independent equally sized groups each have n = 5. Their means are 10, 12 and 15; the significant omnibus ANOVA has \(MS_W=4\) and \(df_W=12\). Use the supplied studentized-range critical value q = 3.773. Calculate HSD and identify the significant pairs.

TipAnswer

\(HSD=3.773\sqrt{4/5}=3.375\). Absolute differences: 2, 5 and 3. Only the pair with means 10 and 15 exceeds HSD.

NoteExplanation

The omnibus test indicates that the means are not all equal, but it gives no pair-specific decision. Tukey’s HSD uses the pooled within-group variability and a critical value for the entire set of pairwise comparisons. The difference of five points exceeds the adjusted threshold; the differences of two and three do not. This avoids turning the global result into an unsupported claim that every pair differs.

Question 29 — Repeated-measures ANOVA (textbook extension)

Eight participants complete four conditions. The total SS is 180, condition SS is 60 and person SS is 84. Find the residual SS, all df, condition F and the decision at .05 (critical F(3,21) = 3.072).

TipAnswer

\(SS_E=180-60-84=36\); \(df_T=31\), \(df_B=3\), \(df_P=7\), \(df_E=21\). \(MS_B=20\), \(MS_E=36/21=1.7143\); \(F(3,21)=11.67\). Reject under the uncorrected repeated-measures assumptions.

NoteExplanation

All 32 scores are used to compute total variability, but the independent units are eight participants. Person SS is separated from the error term because each participant supplies all four conditions. Removing it leaves 36 units of residual SS and 21 residual df. The condition mean square is much larger than the remaining error mean square; inference uses the repeated-measures reference distribution, including its sphericity assumption.

Question 30 — Interpreting a factorial design (textbook extension)

A balanced 2 × 2 between-subjects study has these cell means for a memory task:

Quiet Noise
No strategy 12 8
Strategy 16 12
  1. Identify the two factors and their levels. (b) Calculate their marginal means. (c) Determine the simple strategy effect in each sound condition. (d) Is the observed pattern additive or interactive? Can significance be decided from these means alone?
TipAnswer
  1. Strategy (no strategy, strategy) and sound (quiet, noise). (b) Strategy means 10 and 14; sound means 14 and 10. (c) Strategy improves scores by 4 in both quiet and noise. (d) Additive sample pattern: difference of simple effects = 0. Significance of main effects requires an inferential test using within-cell variability and sample sizes.
NoteExplanation

Because each cell is equally represented, the marginal means average the two cell means for a factor level. The strategy effect is 16 − 12 = 4 in quiet and 12 − 8 = 4 in noise; the two differences are identical. The interaction plot would show parallel lines, even though both factors show nonzero marginal differences. A formal factorial ANOVA tests whether observed patterns provide evidence about population effects, not merely whether the sample means differ.

Week 8 Takeaway

A paired t test asks whether average within-pair change is large relative to its estimated sampling uncertainty. ANOVA asks whether the separation between means is large relative to variability that remains within the relevant design. Both tests use the same broad logic of evidence versus uncertainty, but the research design tells us which quantities count as signal and which belong in the error term.

Match the method to the dependence structure, compare the right sources of variability, and interpret the evidence without claiming more than the test and design support.
