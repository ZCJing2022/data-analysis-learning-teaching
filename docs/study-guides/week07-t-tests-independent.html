Week 07 — t-tests: One-Sample and Independent Measures

Overview

Week 7 builds directly on the hypothesis-testing framework developed in Weeks 5 and 6. Until now, we have tested a sample mean against a population mean using the one-sample z test. That procedure requires us to know the population standard deviation. In psychological research, however, we rarely have that information.

The lecture asks two related questions (Sigal, 2026, slides 4–6, 21–22):

  1. How can we test a sample mean when the population standard deviation is unknown?
  2. How can we test a difference between two independent groups when neither population mean is known?

The first question leads to the one-sample t test. The second leads to the two-independent-sample t test (also called the independent-samples t test). Both use the same basic reasoning: compare an observed statistic with a hypothesized population value, relative to its estimated sampling variability.

The intellectual sequence this week is:

Unknown population variability → Estimated standard error → Student’s t distribution → One-sample t test → Independent group mean differences → Pooled variance and the independent-samples t test → Confidence intervals, effect sizes, and interpretation.

This guide follows the lecture sequence, with Privitera (2018, Chapters 8–9) providing the conceptual and computational support. The 2024 chapters provide supplementary explanations, particularly about assumptions and reading software output. The worksheet is used to reinforce the calculations rather than to introduce a competing workflow.

Learning Objectives

By the end of Week 7, you should be able to:

  1. Explain the relationship between the z test and the t test, and why estimating population variance changes the reference distribution.
  2. Describe the t family, calculate degrees of freedom, and locate critical values using the t table for one- and two-tailed tests.
  3. Distinguish a one-sample comparison with a fixed benchmark from a comparison between two independent samples.
  4. State population hypotheses, compute an estimated standard error and an obtained t statistic, and make a statistical decision.
  5. Calculate and interpret 95% and 99% confidence intervals, and explain their relationship to corresponding two-tailed tests.
  6. Calculate and interpret Cohen’s d for one-sample and pooled independent-samples comparisons; distinguish magnitude from significance.
  7. Explain between-subjects designs, sampling distributions of mean differences, and why independent sources of sampling variance are added.
  8. Compute pooled variance from sums of squares or group variances, including unequal sample sizes, and calculate the pooled test statistic.
  9. Interpret a boxplot and a plot of the mean difference with a confidence interval; calculate the IQR and outlier fences.
  10. Evaluate independence, normality, the measurement scale, and homogeneity of variance; apply the lecture’s variance-ratio heuristics and explain the Welch alternative.
  11. Report a t test in APA style, recognize the limits of causal inference, and distinguish statistical evidence from practical importance.

Part I — From z to Student’s t

1. What Carries Over from Weeks 5 and 6?

The Week 5 sampling distribution describes what would happen if we repeatedly took samples of a fixed size and calculated their means. Its center is the population mean, and its standard deviation is the standard error of the mean. Week 6 used that distribution to evaluate how unusual an observed sample mean would be under a null hypothesis.

When the population standard deviation is known, the one-sample z test uses:

\[ \sigma_{\bar{x}}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad z_{obt}=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma_{\bar{x}}}. \]

Here \(\mu_0\) is the reference mean specified by the null hypothesis. The numerator is the observed departure from that mean; the denominator expresses the typical sampling variability of a mean. We still choose a significance level, determine the number of tails, compare the obtained statistic with the appropriate critical value, and interpret the result in context.

A raw-score z from Week 4 is different: it divides an individual score’s departure by \(\sigma\), not by the standard error. Do not confuse standardizing an individual with testing a sample mean.

2. When the Population Standard Deviation Is Unknown

In many studies, a benchmark mean is available, but the variability of the population is not. We can use the sample variance to estimate the population variance:

\[ SS=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})^2,\qquad s^2=\frac{SS}{n-1},\qquad s=\sqrt{s^2}. \]

Under independent random sampling, \(s^2\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\): over repeated samples its expected value equals the population variance. That does not mean any particular sample variance is correct. Nor is the square root \(s\) exactly unbiased for \(\sigma\). The important point is that we now estimate the variability from the same sample used to calculate the mean (Privitera, 2018, Section 8.1).

The estimated standard error is therefore:

\[ s_{\bar{x}}=\frac{s}{\sqrt n}=\sqrt{\frac{s^2}{n}}. \]

The resulting test statistic is

\[ t_{obt}=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s_{\bar{x}}}. \]

The textbook uses \(M\) for \(\bar{x}\), \(SD\) for \(s\), and \(s_M\) for \(s_{\bar{x}}\). These are notation differences, not different methods.

3. The t Distribution and Degrees of Freedom

William Sealy Gosset, publishing as Student in 1908, developed the t statistic to handle unknown population variability (Privitera, 2018, Section 8.1). The t distribution is a family of symmetric, bell-shaped probability distributions centered at zero. Compared with the standard normal distribution, finite-df t distributions have heavier tails and a lower central peak. A sample may underestimate the true standard deviation, so large standardized departures are more plausible than they would be under the z distribution.

The shape depends on the degrees of freedom (\(df\)). In a one-sample test:

\[ df=n-1. \]

Why subtract one? Once the sample mean is fixed, the deviations from that mean must sum to zero. If you know \(n-1\) deviations, the last deviation is determined. For example, if three deviations are \(-2\), \(1\), and an unknown value, the last must be \(1\). Only two are free to vary. This is the same degrees-of-freedom logic used when calculating sample variance.

As \(df\) grows, the estimate of population variance becomes more stable and the t distribution approaches the standard normal distribution. At low \(df\), the heavier tails require more extreme critical values for the same tail probability.

The curves reproduce the conceptual comparison in Lecture 7, slide 9, and Privitera (2018, Figure 8.1), using computed probability densities rather than a screenshot. The shaded tails illustrate the two-tailed 5% critical region for \(df=7\). Try the lecture’s interactive t distribution demonstration by changing the degrees of freedom.

5. z versus t: Similar Reasoning, Different Denominators

Feature One-sample z test One-sample t test
Population SD Known Unknown
Denominator \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) \(s/\sqrt n\)
Reference distribution Standard normal Student’s t
Degrees of freedom for critical value Not needed \(n-1\)
Two-tailed critical magnitude at .05 1.960 Depends on \(df\); greater than 1.960 for finite \(df\)
Question Is the observed sample mean unusually far from the benchmark? Same question, allowing for estimated variability

Both tests require a defensible sampling model. A large sample does not make an unknown population SD known, and there is no automatic rule to switch from t to z at 30 observations. As the sample grows, their critical values simply become increasingly similar.

Part II — The One-Sample t Test

6. When Should We Use It?

Use a one-sample t test when the research question concerns the mean of one population relative to a specified benchmark, and the population variance is unknown. For example, a researcher may compare weekly reading hours among university students with a national reference value. The benchmark is treated as fixed in the one-sample model; if it is itself a noisy estimate from another sample and that uncertainty matters, a two-sample comparison or other model may be needed.

For a nondirectional question:

\[ H_0:\mu=\mu_0, \qquad H_1:\mu\ne\mu_0. \]

For a directional prediction made before inspecting the data, the alternative could be \(\mu>\mu_0\) or \(\mu<\mu_0\), with the corresponding upper or lower rejection region. The course’s default is a two-tailed test at \(\alpha=.05\) unless otherwise specified.

7. Worked Example — Leisure Reading

Lecture 7, slides 14–17, gives a national benchmark of 4.3 hours per week and the following eight SFU student reading times:

Participant 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8
Hours 7 9 2 6 13 10 4 5

Step 1 — State hypotheses. \(H_0:\mu_{student}=4.3\); \(H_1:\mu_{student}\ne4.3\).

Step 2 — Determine the critical region. \(\alpha=.05\), two-tailed; \(df=8-1=7\); \(t_{crit}=\pm2.365\).

Step 3 — Gather and summarize the data.

\[ \bar{x}=\frac{56}{8}=7,\qquad SS=88, \]

\[ s=\sqrt{\frac{88}{7}}=3.5456,\qquad s_{\bar{x}}=\frac{3.5456}{\sqrt8}=1.2536. \]

Step 4 — Compute the statistic.

\[ t(7)=\frac{7-4.3}{1.2536}=2.154. \]

Step 5 — Make a decision and interpret. Since \(|t|=2.154\) is less than 2.365, fail to reject \(H_0\). The sample reads more on average, but the evidence is insufficient to conclude that the target student population differs from 4.3 hours per week at the .05 level. Software gives \(p\approx.068\) (two-tailed).

NoteA useful distinction

The observed difference is 2.7 hours, not zero. Failing to reject the null is not proof that the two population means are identical. With only eight students, the estimate is relatively uncertain.

8. Point Estimates, Confidence Intervals, and Test Decisions

A point estimate is a single statistic used to estimate a population parameter. Here the point estimate of the population mean is 7 hours, not the test statistic. An interval estimate reports uncertainty around the point estimate. The general form for a one-sample t interval is:

\[ CI_{100(1-\alpha)\%}=\bar{x}\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,df}\,s_{\bar{x}}. \]

The positive critical magnitude multiplied by SE is the margin of error. The lower confidence limit subtracts this margin; the upper limit adds it.

For the reading example, using the table’s 2.365 and retaining precision in SE:

\[ CI_{95}=7\pm2.365(1.253566)\approx[4.04,9.96]. \]

Slide 17 reports approximately 4.04–9.95 after intermediate rounding. The difference is immaterial to the conclusion: the benchmark 4.3 is inside the interval. Thus the corresponding two-tailed test fails to reject \(H_0\).

If we estimate the difference from the benchmark rather than the raw population mean, subtract 4.3 from each endpoint: approximately \([-0.26,5.66]\) hours. This interval includes zero. This distinction explains why some software reports a one-sample CI for the mean difference while others report a CI for the mean itself (Privitera, 2018, Sections 8.6–8.8).

A 95% confidence procedure would produce intervals containing the true fixed parameter in approximately 95% of repeated samples under the model. It does not say that 95% of individual scores are within this interval, nor that the fixed parameter has a 95% probability of being in this particular frequentist interval. A 99% interval uses a larger critical value and is wider for the same data. At fixed SD and confidence, increasing sample size reduces SE and generally narrows the interval.

9. Assumptions and Cohen’s d

The lecture requires independent observations and an approximately normal population. The textbook adds random sampling as a design assumption and emphasizes an interval- or ratio-scale outcome (Privitera, 2018, Section 8.4; 2024, Section 9.4). Check independence from the research design, not from a graph or a p value. Evaluate shape and outliers, especially with small samples. Large independent samples improve the normal approximation for means under suitable conditions, but severe skew or influential observations still warrant care.

Statistical significance addresses evidence against a null claim. Cohen’s d estimates the size of the observed standardized difference:

\[ d=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s}. \]

The denominator is the sample SD, not the estimated standard error. Indeed \(t=d\sqrt n\). For the reading example \(d=2.7/3.5456\approx0.76\). Using the lecture’s reference points, this is near the large-effect benchmark even though the test is nonsignificant.

Absolute magnitude of \(d\) Conventional reference point (slide 18)
about .20 Small
about .50 Medium
.80 or greater Large

These are guides, not universal cutoffs for practical importance. The sign describes the direction. A large standardized difference can still be imprecise, and a small effect can matter in context. The 2018 and 2024 textbooks use somewhat different broad labeling bands; for PSYC 210 use the lecture’s .20/.50/.80 anchors. Slide 45 explicitly excludes eta-squared and omega-squared from the assigned t-test study scope, so this guide does not require them.

10. Worked Example — CARS Scores

Lecture 7, slides 19–20, asks whether the following scores differ from a population benchmark of 75:

\[ 27,82,32,57,50,61,55,88,57,60. \]

The hypotheses are \(H_0:\mu=75\) and \(H_1:\mu\ne75\). The group has \(n=10\) and \(df=9\). At .05, two-tailed, the critical values are \(\pm2.262\).

\[ \bar{x}=56.9,\quad s=18.8234,\quad s_{\bar{x}}=5.9525, \]

\[ t(9)=\frac{56.9-75}{5.9525}=-3.041. \]

The obtained value falls in the lower rejection region. Reject \(H_0\); the CARS scores provide evidence of a lower population mean than 75 (two-tailed \(p\approx.014\)). The 95% CI for the population mean is approximately \([43.43,70.37]\), entirely below 75. The estimated effect size is \(d=-18.1/18.8234\approx-0.96\), a large magnitude in the lecture’s convention. This is a comparison with the given benchmark; the slide does not establish that a specific intervention caused the difference.

Part III — Comparing Two Independent Group Means

11. From a Benchmark to Another Sample

Sometimes no fixed reference mean exists. We may instead compare a treatment group with a control group, two classes, or two groups exposed to different study methods. The same quantitative outcome is measured for each group. In a between-subjects design, different participants contribute observations to each condition (Privitera, 2018, Sections 9.1–9.2).

There are two common ways to obtain independent groups:

  • Sample separately from preexisting groups or populations, as when comparing students who already attend different classes. This can provide evidence of a difference, but preexisting differences may confound its interpretation.
  • Randomly assign sampled participants to two different conditions. When treatment is manipulated and alternatives are adequately controlled, random assignment strengthens causal interpretation. Random assignment is different from random sampling, which concerns the population to which results can generalize.

A before-and-after measurement of the same participants is not an independent-groups design. It requires the related-samples method introduced next week. Rows printed side by side in an independent-group data table do not establish pairs.

12. Hypotheses and the Sampling Distribution of Mean Differences

For two independent populations, the usual null hypothesis is that their means are equal:

\[ H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=0,\qquad H_1:\mu_1-\mu_2\ne0. \]

Equivalently, \(H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2\). These are claims about population means, not about whether the observed sample means happen to match. The general structure of a t statistic is

\[ t=\frac{\text{observed statistic}-\text{hypothesized parameter}}{\text{estimated standard error}}. \]

For two groups, the observed statistic is \(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2\). The sampling distribution of this difference is the distribution we would obtain by repeatedly drawing independent samples of the same respective sizes and subtracting their means. Its center is \(\mu_1-\mu_2\), which is zero under the usual null hypothesis. The uncertainty now comes from both samples.

\[ s_{\bar{x}_1}=\frac{s_1}{\sqrt{n_1}},\qquad s_{\bar{x}_2}=\frac{s_2}{\sqrt{n_2}}. \]

For independent means, their sampling variances add, even when the means are subtracted. Without imposing a common variance, the estimate is

\[ SE_{unpooled}=\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}. \]

Do not add SDs or SEs directly. Square the separate SEs, add them, and take the square root. This unpooled SE is the basis for Welch’s test, discussed in Part IV. The main hand-calculation procedure in the lecture uses a pooled estimate under the equal-variance assumption.

13. Why Pool the Variances?

Suppose the two populations have a common variance. Each group supplies a separate estimate of that same quantity. Larger groups usually provide more information about within-group variability, so the pooled sample variance gives greater weight to the estimate with more degrees of freedom (Privitera, 2018, Section 9.4).

\[ s_p^2=\frac{SS_1+SS_2}{df_1+df_2} =\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}. \]

If group sizes are equal, this simplifies to \((s_1^2+s_2^2)/2\). Pool variances, not the standard deviations, and calculate deviations around each group’s own mean. Pooling all scores around a grand mean would mix the between-group difference into the within-group variance.

For example, if \(n_1=6\), \(s_1^2=9\), \(n_2=16\), and \(s_2^2=16\):

\[ s_p^2=\frac{5(9)+15(16)}{20}=14.25. \]

The result lies between 9 and 16 and is closer to the larger group’s variance. Equal sample sizes are not a requirement of the pooled test; a common population variance is.

14. The Pooled Independent-Samples t Test

The pooled estimated standard error of the difference is

\[ s_{(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)}=\sqrt{\frac{s_p^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_p^2}{n_2}}. \]

Thus, with the usual zero null difference,

\[ t_{obt}=\frac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{s_{(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)}},\qquad df=(n_1-1)+(n_2-1)=n_1+n_2-2. \]

We lose one degree of freedom in each group because each mean is estimated separately. With \(N=n_1+n_2\), the textbook writes this as \(N-2\). Select \(t_{crit}\) from the table using this total \(df\), the significance level, and the tails. The sign of the statistic follows the subtraction order; reversing the group order reverses the sign of t and d, but leaves the two-tailed p value unchanged.

\(n\) 10 10

Step 1. \(H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=0\); \(H_1:\mu_1-\mu_2\ne0\).

Step 2. \(\alpha=.05\), two-tailed; \(df=10+10-2=18\); \(t_{crit}=\pm2.101\).

Step 3. Compute the pooled variance, SE, and t:

\[ s_p^2=\frac{200+160}{9+9}=20, \]

\[ SE=\sqrt{\frac{20}{10}+\frac{20}{10}}=2, \]

\[ t(18)=\frac{25-19}{2}=3.00. \]

Step 4. Since \(3.00>2.101\), reject \(H_0\). The experimental group recalled significantly more pairs than the control group (two-tailed \(p\approx.0077\)). The observed mean difference is six pairs. Whether this difference can be attributed causally to mental imagery depends on the design and comparability of the groups, not on the test statistic alone.

16. Mean Difference, Confidence Interval, and Effect Size

The point estimate is the sample mean difference, 6. Its confidence interval uses the same estimated standard error as the test:

\[ CI_{95}=(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)\pm t_{crit}SE =6\pm2.101(2)=[1.798,10.202]. \]

Zero is outside this interval. The corresponding two-tailed pooled test rejects the null at .05. The data support a positive difference, with a range of plausible population mean differences of roughly 1.80–10.20 recalled pairs under the model.

The estimated Cohen’s d for the pooled comparison is

\[ d=\frac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{s_p},\qquad s_p=\sqrt{s_p^2}. \]

\[ d=\frac{6}{\sqrt{20}}=1.342. \]

This is a large standardized difference. Notice that the denominator of t is 2 (SE of the difference), whereas the denominator of d is 4.472 (pooled within-group SD). They answer different questions. With fixed group means and variances, increasing both sample sizes shrinks SE and increases the magnitude of t, while the estimated d remains unchanged. Actual estimates can change when new observations are collected.

Part IV — Visualizing, Checking, and Reporting Group Differences

17. Reading a Boxplot

Lecture 7, slide 37, explains the boxplot from the center outward. The line inside the box is the median, the bottom and top of the box represent the first and third quartiles, and the box spans the middle 50% of observations.

\[ IQR=Q_3-Q_1,\quad LF=Q_1-1.5IQR,\quad UF=Q_3+1.5IQR. \]

The whiskers extend to the smallest and largest observed values within the fences. They do not necessarily end exactly at the fences or at the overall minimum and maximum. Points beyond a fence are flagged as potential outliers; a flag is not a reason by itself to delete a score. The mean can be added as a separate symbol, as on slide 38, but is not the usual line inside the box. Different software may compute quartiles slightly differently, so use the specified course convention when calculating quartiles by hand.

In the lecture’s imagery example, the experimental group has a higher mean and median and slightly greater spread. The groups’ score ranges overlap, yet the mean difference is statistically significant. Privitera (2018, Figure 9.2) illustrates why the same mean difference can be more or less detectable depending on within-group variability. Overlap alone cannot determine significance; sample sizes and the sampling error of the difference also matter.

18. Plotting a Mean Difference with its Confidence Interval

This visualization represents Lecture 7, slide 39, using the recomputed mean difference and confidence limits. It directly shows the estimated difference (six pairs) and the uncertainty surrounding it. The zero line indicates the null difference. Because the entire interval is above zero, the conclusion agrees with the two-tailed test. A boxplot depicts the spread of individual scores; the confidence interval depicts uncertainty about the mean difference. They are complementary rather than interchangeable.

19. Assumptions of the Pooled Independent-Samples Test

The lecture’s technical profile (slides 40–41) and the textbook (2018, Section 9.4; 2024, Section 10.4) identify these conditions:

Condition What to check Why it matters
Two independent groups Study design; no repeated measurements or matched pairs analyzed as independent Dependence changes the sampling variance
Quantitative outcome Comparable interval/ratio or approximately continuous scores in both groups The mean difference must be meaningful
Random sampling / defensible sampling model Recruitment, design, target population Determines the scope of inference
Approximate normality Examine each group separately using plots, skewness and outliers Especially important with small groups; severe departures can distort inference
Homogeneity of variance Compare within-group variances The pooled model assumes a common population variance

The lecture recommends considering a transformation for substantial non-normality. If a transformation is justified, apply the same transformation to both groups and interpret the difference on the transformed scale. Do not transform only one group, or remove outliers solely to obtain significance. Welch addresses unequal variances, not dependence or arbitrary non-normality.

The course’s variance-ratio heuristic is

\[ R=\frac{s_{max}^2}{s_{min}^2}. \]

For equal sample sizes use \(R<4\); for unequal sample sizes use \(R<2\) (slide 41). For the imagery data, \(R=22.222/17.778=1.25\), consistent with the heuristic. These cutoffs are screening rules, not proof of equal population variances. The textbook offers the more general rule of about 2; for class exercises, follow the lecture’s distinction between equal and unequal sample sizes. If the smaller variance is zero, the ratio is undefined or infinite and requires special attention.

20. Welch’s Correction for Heterogeneous Variances

Slide 42 introduces Welch’s unpooled t test. When a common variance is not a reasonable assumption, use separate estimates of variance and a corrected degrees of freedom:

\[ t'=\frac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}}, \]

\[ df_{Welch}=\frac{(s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2)^2}{\frac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1}+\frac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}}. \]

The corrected degrees of freedom can be noninteger, and software handles this directly. Welch’s degrees of freedom are at most \(n_1+n_2-2\) (for positive variance estimates). The slide’s statement that equal sample variances imply the uncorrected df needs qualification: equality also holds for the equal-size/equal-variance case; equal variances alone do not guarantee it when sample sizes differ. Equal sample sizes make the pooled and unpooled SEs identical, even when sample variances differ, but their degrees of freedom may still differ.

Illustration: suppose group 1 has \(n_1=10\), \(\bar{x}_1=30\), \(s_1=6\); group 2 has \(n_2=20\), \(\bar{x}_2=25\), \(s_2=2\). The variance ratio is \(36/4=9\), which fails the course’s unequal-size heuristic.

\[ SE_{Welch}=\sqrt{36/10+4/20}=\sqrt{3.8}=1.9494, \]

\[ t'=5/1.9494\approx2.565,\qquad df_{Welch}\approx10.01. \]

The appropriate critical magnitude is approximately 2.228 for a two-tailed .05 test (using df 10 as a conservative table row). Reject \(H_0\). The example illustrates why the same pooled formula should not be applied automatically to unequal variances. In actual analysis, Welch is often a sensible default; in hand-calculation exercises, use the method requested by the instructor.

21. Reporting and Reading Software Results

The lecture gives the following reporting structure (slide 44): direction and descriptive statistics, test statistic and degrees of freedom, p value, and effect size. Add the confidence interval and SDs when available. For the imagery example, a fuller APA-style report is:

Participants instructed to form mental images recalled more pairs (\(M=25.00\), \(SD=4.71\)) than control participants (\(M=19.00\), \(SD=4.22\)), \(t(18)=3.00\), \(p=.008\), \(d=1.34\), with a mean difference of 6.00 pairs, 95% CI [1.80, 10.20].

The exact p value here is computed from the t distribution; the lecture reports \(p<.05\). A printed table often allows only a probability range. For example, a two-tailed .05 cutoff of 2.101 and .01 cutoff of 2.878 at \(df=18\) establish that the imagery example has \(p<.01\). Do not read a t critical value as a p value, or use the normal z table for a small-df t statistic.

Worksheet 1 — Comparing z and t

Both use the difference from a hypothesized mean divided by the standard error. The z test requires known population SD; the t test estimates it using the sample SD and uses a df-dependent critical value. The null hypothesis and rejection logic do not otherwise change.

Worksheet 2 — Age at First Word

The benchmark is 13.0 months; a group of 15 children using a technique has mean 11.0 months and SD 3.34 months. Unknown population SD indicates a one-sample t test. For the nondirectional question:

One sample vs fixed benchmark, unknown population SD One-sample t \(s/\sqrt n\) \(n-1\)
Two independent means, common variance assumed Pooled independent t \(\sqrt{s_p^2/n_1+s_p^2/n_2}\) \(n_1+n_2-2\)

The general test logic is observed departure from the null divided by its estimated uncertainty. The research design determines which statistic and denominator are appropriate.

24. Common Mistakes and Exam Preparation

  • Use population hypotheses, not statements about the already-observed sample means.
  • Choose the tails before analyzing results. A negative t can be significant in a two-tailed test.
  • Use \(n-1\) for a one-sample t and \(n_1+n_2-2\) for the pooled independent test, not the total sample size minus one.
  • Use estimated SE for t, and SD (or pooled SD) for Cohen’s d. Pool variances, not SDs.
  • A one-sample mean CI is compared with the benchmark; a mean-difference CI is compared with zero for the usual null.
  • A 99% CI is wider than a 95% CI from the same sample. Greater precision is not the same as greater confidence level.
  • Changing alpha changes the decision boundary, not the obtained t. Holding the observed difference fixed, greater within-group variability tends to reduce the magnitude of t; increasing sample size tends to increase it.
  • Do not interpret boxplot overlap as a significance test. Check variance ratios using variances rather than SDs.
  • Failing to reject a null hypothesis is not evidence of exact equality. Statistical significance and large effect sizes do not establish causation.

Assignment-Style Practice

Question 1 — Why t? (Multiple choice)

A researcher compares the mean anxiety score of 16 students with a fixed reference mean. The population SD is unknown. Which procedure is appropriate?

  • A. One-sample z test using the sample SD
  • B. Pooled independent-samples t test
  • C. One-sample t test
  • D. Related-samples t test
TipAnswer

C. One-sample t test.

NoteExplanation

There is only one observed sample and a fixed comparison mean. Since the population SD is unknown, the researcher estimates the SE from the sample SD. This extra uncertainty calls for a t distribution with \(df=15\), not a standard normal distribution or a test involving two samples.

Question 2 — Degrees of freedom (Multiple choice)

A one-sample t test uses 19 independent observations. What are its degrees of freedom?

  • A. 19
  • B. 18
  • C. 17
  • D. 20
TipAnswer

B. 18. \(df=19-1=18\).

NoteExplanation

The sample mean is estimated from the 19 scores, imposing one constraint on deviations from that mean. Only 18 deviations are free to vary. The test uses the same degrees of freedom as the sample variance used in the estimated SE.

Question 3 — Shape of the reference distribution (Multiple choice)

Compared with the standard normal distribution, a t distribution with 4 degrees of freedom has:

  • A. A higher central peak and lighter tails
  • B. A mean greater than zero
  • C. A total area greater than one
  • D. A lower central peak and heavier tails
TipAnswer

D. A lower central peak and heavier tails.

NoteExplanation

With only four degrees of freedom, the variance estimate is relatively unstable. The reference distribution allocates more probability to extreme standardized values than the normal distribution, so the central peak is lower and tails are heavier. Like the normal distribution, it remains symmetric about zero and has total area one.

Question 4 — Critical values (Multiple choice)

A one-sample t test has \(n=11\) and uses \(\alpha=.05\), two-tailed. Which critical values should be used?

  • A. \(\pm2.228\)
  • B. \(\pm1.960\)
  • C. \(\pm2.262\)
  • D. \(\pm1.812\)
TipAnswer

A. \(\pm2.228\).

NoteExplanation

First calculate \(df=11-1=10\), then look up the column for .05 in two tails combined. The value 2.262 belongs to \(df=9\), and 1.812 is the one-tailed .05 magnitude for \(df=10\). The z cutoff 1.960 does not account for the uncertainty in the estimated SD.

Question 5 — Estimated SE (Multiple choice)

For a sample with \(n=16\) and \(s=12\), what is the estimated standard error of the mean?

  • A. 0.75
  • B. 4
  • C. 12
  • D. 3
TipAnswer

D. 3. \(s_{\bar{x}}=12/\sqrt{16}=3\).

NoteExplanation

The SD of 12 describes variability among individual observations. The estimated SE describes how sample means would vary across repeated samples of 16 observations. Divide the SD by the square root of the sample size, not by the sample size itself.

Question 6 — Negative t and significance (Multiple choice)

A two-tailed test gives \(t(14)=-2.70\). The critical values at .05 are \(\pm2.145\). What is the correct decision?

  • A. Fail to reject \(H_0\) because 2.70 exceeds 2.145
  • B. Fail to reject \(H_0\) because t is negative
  • C. Reject \(H_0\)
  • D. Change to a one-tailed test after seeing the result
TipAnswer

C. Reject \(H_0\).

NoteExplanation

The two rejection regions lie beyond the negative and positive critical boundaries. The obtained statistic is more negative than \(-2.145\), or equivalently its absolute magnitude exceeds 2.145. The negative sign describes the direction of the observed difference; it does not make the result nonsignificant.

Question 7 — Confidence interval (Multiple choice)

A one-sample 95% CI for the population mean is [18, 26]. The null hypothesis specifies a mean of 20. What does the corresponding two-tailed .05 test conclude?

  • A. Reject because the point estimate is 22
  • B. Fail to reject because 20 is inside the interval
  • C. Prove that the population mean equals 20
  • D. Reject because zero is outside the interval
TipAnswer

B. Fail to reject because 20 is inside the interval.

NoteExplanation

The interval estimates the population mean itself, so the relevant null value is 20, not zero. The interval contains 20, making the observed data compatible with the null at the corresponding two-sided significance level. This does not establish that 20 is the exact population mean.

Question 8 — Choosing a design (Multiple choice)

Which comparison calls for an independent-samples t test rather than a related-samples test, assuming the quantitative outcome and other conditions are suitable?

  • A. The same students before and after a memory course
  • B. Two randomly assigned groups using different memory strategies
  • C. Each participant’s left-hand and right-hand reaction times
  • D. Matched pairs of twins analyzed by their within-pair differences
TipAnswer

B. Two randomly assigned groups using different memory strategies.

NoteExplanation

Each person contributes to one of two distinct groups, so the group means can be treated as independent when the design supports this assumption. The other examples contain repeated or matched measurements whose dependence should be reflected in the analysis. The choice of test begins with the design rather than the number of columns in a spreadsheet.

Question 9 — Pooled variance (Multiple choice)

Group 1 has \(n_1=5\), \(s_1^2=12\); group 2 has \(n_2=13\), \(s_2^2=16\). What is the pooled variance?

  • A. 15.00
  • B. 3.87
  • C. 14.00
  • D. 28.00
TipAnswer

A. 15.00. \(s_p^2=[4(12)+12(16)]/16=15\).

NoteExplanation

Under the common-variance model, the two within-group variances estimate one population variance. The second group has more degrees of freedom, so its variance receives three times the weight of the first. Simply averaging the two variances gives 14, ignoring the unequal amounts of information; 3.87 is the pooled SD, not the variance.

Question 10 — Homogeneity of variance (Multiple choice)

Two independent groups have unequal sample sizes and sample variances 18 and 6. What does the lecture’s variance-ratio heuristic suggest?

  • A. Ratio = 12; sample sizes do not matter
  • B. Ratio = 3; the unequal-size heuristic is satisfied
  • C. Ratio = 1/3; the variances are equal
  • D. Ratio = 3; the unequal-size heuristic is not satisfied
TipAnswer

D. Ratio = 3; the unequal-size heuristic is not satisfied.

NoteExplanation

Divide the larger variance by the smaller: \(18/6=3\). With unequal group sizes the lecture uses a ratio less than 2 as its screening criterion, rather than the less-than-4 criterion for equal group sizes. The result suggests considering Welch’s test; it does not by itself establish the true population variances.

Question 11 — Cohen’s d (Multiple choice)

Two independent groups differ by 8 points. The pooled variance is 100 and the estimated SE of the mean difference is 2.5. What is pooled Cohen’s d?

  • A. 3.20
  • B. 0.08
  • C. 0.80
  • D. 1.25
TipAnswer

C. 0.80. \(d=8/\sqrt{100}=0.80\).

NoteExplanation

Cohen’s d expresses the raw mean difference in pooled within-group SD units. The pooled SD is 10, so the difference is eight-tenths of a standard deviation, at the lecture’s large-effect reference point. Dividing by 2.5 instead would calculate t, which measures the difference relative to uncertainty in the estimated mean difference.

Question 12 — Reading a boxplot (Multiple choice)

In a standard boxplot without an added mean symbol, what does the line inside the box represent?

  • A. The median
  • B. The mean
  • C. The standard error
  • D. The lower outlier fence
TipAnswer

A. The median.

NoteExplanation

The central line marks the 50th percentile, while the edges of the box mark the 25th and 75th percentiles. The whiskers extend to the most extreme observed values within the calculated fences. A mean marker may be added separately, as in the lecture, but the line in a conventional boxplot is not the mean or a confidence interval.

Question 13 — Full one-sample test (Constructed response)

A sample of nine independent participants completes a concentration task. The mean score is 46, the sample SD is 6, and the benchmark mean is 50. Use \(t_{crit}=\pm2.306\).

State hypotheses, compute df, SE, t and the 95% CI for the mean. Make a decision and estimate Cohen’s d.

TipAnswer

\(H_0:\mu=50\), \(H_1:\mu\ne50\); \(df=8\).

\[ SE=6/\sqrt9=2,\qquad t(8)=(46-50)/2=-2.00. \]

\[ CI_{95}=46\pm2.306(2)=[41.39,50.61]. \]

Fail to reject \(H_0\). \(d=-4/6=-0.67\) (between medium and large reference points).

NoteExplanation

The observed mean is four points below the reference, but the relevant comparison is how large this gap is relative to the estimated SE. The statistic is two estimated SEs below the null mean, which is not beyond the critical boundary at eight degrees of freedom. The confidence interval includes 50 and therefore gives the same decision. The effect estimate is appreciable in SD units, but the small sample leaves considerable uncertainty.

Question 14 — From raw observations to a t statistic (Constructed response)

Five students report study times of 2, 4, 4, 6, and 9 hours. Test the mean against a fixed benchmark of 3 hours, using \(t_{crit}=\pm2.776\). Compute the mean, SS, sample variance, SD, SE and t.

TipAnswer

\[ \bar{x}=25/5=5,\quad SS=9+1+1+1+16=28, \]

\[ s^2=28/4=7,\quad s=\sqrt7=2.646,\quad SE=\sqrt{7/5}=1.183. \]

\[ t(4)=(5-3)/1.183=1.690. \]

Fail to reject \(H_0:\mu=3\) against \(H_1:\mu\ne3\).

NoteExplanation

Begin with the sample mean because it is the center from which the squared deviations are computed. Dividing SS by four rather than five produces the sample variance used to estimate the unknown population variance. The resulting SE converts the two-hour difference into estimated sampling-error units. Although the observed mean is higher, a statistic of 1.690 is not sufficiently extreme for this small sample at the stated alpha.

Question 15 — One-tailed and two-tailed decisions (Constructed response)

Before collecting data, a researcher predicts that a population mean will exceed 40. A one-sample test yields \(t(9)=2.00\). For \(df=9\), the upper-tail .05 critical value is 1.833 and the two-tailed .05 critical magnitude is 2.262. What are the two decisions? Can the researcher choose the tail based on these results?

TipAnswer

Upper-tailed: reject \(H_0\) at .05 because \(2.00>1.833\). Two-tailed: fail to reject because \(|2.00|<2.262\). Choose the test direction before inspecting the results.

NoteExplanation

A justified directional alternative places the entire alpha probability in the predicted tail, so its positive threshold is lower than that of a two-tailed test. The nondirectional test splits the same alpha between both tails and requires a larger absolute statistic. Switching to a one-tailed test after observing a favorable result would invalidate the prespecified decision rule and its advertised error control.

Question 16 — Confidence level and sample size (Constructed response)

A sample has \(\bar{x}=32\), \(s=8\), and \(n=16\). Compute 95% and 99% CIs with critical magnitudes 2.131 and 2.947. If sample size increased while mean and SD stayed fixed, what would happen to the intervals?

TipAnswer

\(df=15\); \(SE=8/4=2\).

\[ CI_{95}=32\pm4.262=[27.74,36.26], \]

\[ CI_{99}=32\pm5.894=[26.11,37.89]. \]

With larger n, both intervals would become narrower, holding mean, SD, and confidence levels fixed.

NoteExplanation

Increasing confidence from 95% to 99% changes the critical magnitude, not the estimated mean or SE. A larger margin of error is needed to attain the higher long-run coverage. Increasing sample size instead reduces SE and moves the critical value closer to the normal cutoff, improving precision for the same confidence level.

Question 17 — What changes t and d? (Constructed response)

A study has \(\bar{x}-\mu_0=4\), \(s=8\), \(n=25\). Calculate t and d. Repeat with \(n=100\) while holding the observed difference and SD fixed. Would changing alpha from .05 to .01 alter either obtained statistic?

TipAnswer

At \(n=25\): \(SE=1.6\), \(t=2.5\), \(d=.50\).

At \(n=100\): \(SE=.8\), \(t=5.0\), \(d=.50\).

Changing alpha changes the rejection threshold, not t or d.

NoteExplanation

Quadrupling n halves the estimated SE and therefore doubles t when the observed difference and SD are fixed. Cohen’s d has no SE or explicit sample-size term in its denominator, so it remains .50 in this hypothetical comparison. This demonstrates why a larger sample can yield stronger statistical evidence without a larger observed standardized effect. In practice, collecting new data may also change the observed mean and SD.

Question 18 — Two independent groups: degrees of freedom and SE (Constructed response)

Two independent groups have \(n_1=8\), \(SS_1=63\) and \(n_2=12\), \(SS_2=99\). Compute both sample variances, pooled variance, total df, and pooled SE of the difference.

TipAnswer

\[ s_1^2=63/7=9,\quad s_2^2=99/11=9,\quad df=7+11=18, \]

\[ s_p^2=(63+99)/18=9, \]

\[ SE=\sqrt{9/8+9/12}=\sqrt{1.875}=1.369. \]

NoteExplanation

Although the two sums of squares differ, dividing each by its degrees of freedom reveals equal observed variances. Pooling them produces the same common estimate of nine. Both means contribute sampling error, with the smaller sample contributing more through its smaller denominator. Add their estimated sampling variances and take the square root, rather than adding the two standard errors.

Question 19 — Full pooled independent-samples analysis (Constructed response)

Two independent groups each contain 10 people. Group 1 has mean 72 and sample variance 25; group 2 has mean 67 and sample variance 20. Assume common population variance and use \(t_{crit}=\pm2.101\). State hypotheses and compute \(df\), pooled variance, SE, t, the 95% CI for the difference, Cohen’s d, and the decision.

TipAnswer

\(H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=0\), \(H_1:\mu_1-\mu_2\ne0\); \(df=18\).

\[ s_p^2=(25+20)/2=22.5,\qquad SE=\sqrt{22.5/10+22.5/10}=\sqrt{4.5}=2.121. \]

\[ t(18)=5/2.121=2.357,\qquad CI_{95}=5\pm2.101(2.121)\approx[0.54,9.46]. \]

\[ d=5/\sqrt{22.5}=1.054. \]

Reject \(H_0\); group 1 has a higher mean, with a large standardized difference.

NoteExplanation

Equal group sizes make the pooled variance the arithmetic average of the two sample variances, but the estimated SE must still incorporate both sample sizes. The resulting absolute t exceeds the two-tailed threshold and the confidence interval excludes zero. Cohen’s d uses the pooled SD rather than SE, showing that the observed gap is slightly more than one within-group SD. The causal meaning of the result depends on how the groups were formed.

Question 20 — Welch correction (Constructed response)

Two independent groups have \(n_1=8\), \(\bar{x}_1=20\), \(s_1^2=16\) and \(n_2=12\), \(\bar{x}_2=16\), \(s_2^2=4\). Calculate the variance ratio, Welch SE, t, and approximate Welch df. Use a conservative table critical magnitude of 2.306 at \(df=8\) to decide at .05.

TipAnswer

\[ R=16/4=4,\qquad SE=\sqrt{16/8+4/12}=1.528, \]

\[ t'=4/1.528=2.619, \]

\[ df_{Welch}=\frac{(2+1/3)^2}{2^2/7+(1/3)^2/11}\approx9.36. \]

The unequal-size ratio heuristic fails. Reject \(H_0\) using even the conservative supplied critical magnitude 2.306. The exact df can be used by software.

NoteExplanation

Because the sample variances differ substantially and the sample sizes are unequal, the lecture recommends considering an unpooled analysis. Welch retains the two group-specific variance contributions rather than forcing a common variance. Its corrected df is below the pooled df of 18; using a conservative lower table row still yields rejection here. Welch does not repair a failure of independence or guarantee valid inference under severe non-normality.

Question 21 — Boxplot fences (Constructed response)

A dataset has \(Q_1=12\), median 15, and \(Q_3=18\). Calculate IQR and both outlier fences. If observations include 3, 4, 26, and 29, which are flagged as potential outliers? Does that automatically justify removing them?

TipAnswer

\[ IQR=18-12=6,\quad LF=12-1.5(6)=3,\quad UF=18+1.5(6)=27. \]

Only 29 is outside the fences. A value equal to the lower fence (3) is not beyond it. Investigate flagged values rather than automatically deleting them.

NoteExplanation

The IQR measures the span of the middle half of the observations and is relatively resistant to extremes. The 1.5-IQR rule identifies observations unusually distant from the central box; the whiskers stop at observed values within the fences. A flagged observation could be a valid part of the population, a recording error, or an indication that the distribution is skewed. Its treatment requires justification from the data and research context.

Question 22 — Read a confidence interval for the difference (Constructed response)

A pooled independent-samples analysis reports a 95% CI of [-1.5, 5.5] for group 1 minus group 2. Identify the point estimate, margin of error, and corresponding two-tailed decision. What would happen to the interval if the group order were reversed?

TipAnswer

Point estimate \(=(-1.5+5.5)/2=2\). Margin of error \(=(5.5-(-1.5))/2=3.5\). Fail to reject the zero-difference null at .05. Reversed order: estimate \(-2\); CI [-5.5, 1.5]; same two-tailed decision.

NoteExplanation

The ordinary t interval is symmetric around the observed difference. Because the original interval spans zero, both positive and negative population mean differences remain compatible with the data under the stated procedure. Reversing the subtraction changes the signs of both endpoints and the estimate, while the uncertainty and two-sided significance conclusion stay the same. It is not a demonstration that the groups are equal.

Question 23 — Interpreting and reporting a result (Constructed response)

Two independent samples show \(M_1=18\), \(SD_1=4\), \(M_2=14\), \(SD_2=4\) with \(n_1=n_2=18\). The pooled test reports \(t(34)=3.00\), two-tailed \(p=.005\), and 95% CI [1.29, 6.71] for the difference. Write a concise APA-style report including Cohen’s d. If the groups were defined by existing study habits, what causal limitation applies?

TipAnswer

\(d=(18-14)/4=1.00\).

Group 1 (\(M=18.00\), \(SD=4.00\)) scored higher than group 2 (\(M=14.00\), \(SD=4.00\)), \(t(34)=3.00\), \(p=.005\), \(d=1.00\), mean difference = 4.00, 95% CI [1.29, 6.71]. The preexisting group design does not establish that study habits caused the difference.

NoteExplanation

A useful report states the direction and magnitude in original units, includes the test statistic, df and p value, and communicates both standardized magnitude and uncertainty. The interval excludes zero, consistent with significance, and the pooled SD of four makes the effect one SD in size. None of these statistics removes possible confounding from a comparison of preexisting groups; causal interpretation requires additional design evidence.

Question 24 — Assumptions and test selection (Constructed response)

For each scenario, identify the appropriate starting method or concern.

  1. One independent sample is compared with a fixed benchmark, population SD unknown.

  2. Different people in two independent groups have very unequal variances.

  3. The same participants are tested before and after an intervention.

  4. The two groups have severely skewed distributions with small sample sizes.

  5. A Shapiro–Wilk test gives \(p=.40\) and Levene’s test gives \(p=.30\). Does this prove that the model assumptions hold?

TipAnswer
  1. One-sample t test; (b) Welch independent-samples t test; (c) related-samples test (next week); (d) examine distributions, outliers, and the design, considering a justified common transformation or alternative method; (e) no, neither nonsignificant diagnostic proves the corresponding assumption.
NoteExplanation

The design establishes whether the statistical object is a single mean, a difference between independent means, or a mean of paired differences. Welch changes the variance model for independent groups but does not address the dependence in repeated measurements. Small samples with severe skew can make t-based inference unreliable, so it is important to inspect both groups rather than rely on a mechanical threshold. Diagnostic tests provide evidence about assumptions but have limited ability to detect violations, especially with small samples.

25. Self-Check Before Moving to Week 8

You should be able to explain without notes:

  • why estimating the unknown population variance leads to Student’s t;
  • what determines the shape of the t distribution and which row and column to use in the t table;
  • why the standard error of independent mean differences incorporates both samples;
  • why pooled variance is weighted by within-group degrees of freedom;
  • why the pooled t and Cohen’s d use different denominators;
  • how to decide from a t statistic or a matching confidence interval;
  • how boxplots, variance-ratio checks, and the Welch alternative support an appropriate analysis;
  • why the same participants measured twice require a different method;
  • how to communicate an effect in context without claiming more than the design supports.

Week 7 Takeaway

The shift from z to t does not change the basic purpose of hypothesis testing. It changes how we account for uncertainty when population variability must be estimated. Comparing two independent groups then extends that same reasoning to a difference between two uncertain means.

Choose the method from the research design, estimate the appropriate sampling variability, and interpret significance alongside effect size and uncertainty.
