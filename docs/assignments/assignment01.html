Question 1 — Summation Notation

NoteOriginal question

1. (/6) Given

\[ x_i=\{16,14,6,11,19,18,17,6\} \]

and

\[ y_i=\{14,-4,14,9,15,14,2,14\}, \]

calculate:

a. \(\sum(x_i)\) b. \(\sum(y_i)\) c. \([\sum(x_i)]^2\)
d. \([\sum(y_i)]^2\) e. \(\sum(x_i-y_i)\) f. \(\sum(x_iy_i)\)
g. \(\sum(x_i)^2\) h. \(\sum(y_i)^2\) i. \([\sum(x_i-y_i)]^2\)
j. \((\sum x_i)(\sum y_i)\) k. \(\sum(x_i+3)^2\) l. \(\sum(y_i/2^2)\)

What this question is asking you to do

This question tests whether you can read summation notation correctly and apply the operations in the correct order. The main challenge is not arithmetic; it is deciding what is done first.

Several expressions look very similar but mean different things. For example:

  • \([\sum(x_i)]^2\) means sum first, then square the total.
  • \(\sum(x_i)^2\) means square each \(x_i\) value first, then sum.
  • \(\sum(x_i-y_i)\) means subtract within each paired row, then sum.
  • \((\sum x_i)(\sum y_i)\) means find two separate sums, then multiply them.

How to approach it

  1. Write the two vectors in parallel so that each \(x_i\) stays paired with the corresponding \(y_i\).
  2. For each subquestion, translate the notation into words before calculating.
  3. Use BEDMAS/PEDMAS:
    • brackets/parentheses;
    • exponents;
    • multiplication/division;
    • addition/subtraction.
  4. For a and b, simply add the values in the requested vector.
  5. For c and d, calculate the sum first and square only after the summation is complete.
  6. For e, calculate each paired difference \(x_i-y_i\), then add the differences.
  7. For f, calculate each paired product \(x_iy_i\), then add the products.
  8. For g and h, square each individual value first, then sum the squared values.
  9. For i, calculate the paired differences, sum those differences, then square the final sum.
  10. For j, calculate \(\sum x_i\) and \(\sum y_i\) separately, then multiply the two totals.
  11. For k, add 3 to each \(x_i\), square each transformed value, then sum.
  12. For l, pay close attention to the denominator: evaluate \(2^2\) according to order of operations, apply the division to each \(y_i\), and then sum.

Question 2 — Adding a Constant to Every Score

NoteOriginal question

2. (/2) If an instructor adds a constant bonus of 5 points to every student’s exam score in a class, what happens to the class mean and what happens to the class standard deviation? Briefly explain why the standard deviation behaves the way it does.

What this question is asking you to do

You need to explain how adding the same constant to every score affects:

  1. the center of the distribution, represented by the mean; and
  2. the spread of the distribution, represented by the standard deviation.

The explanation matters. You should not only state what happens; you should show that you understand why.

How to approach it

Represent the transformed score as:

\[ Y_i=X_i+5. \]

Then consider the two statistics separately.

For the mean, ask: if every score is shifted upward by exactly the same amount, what happens to the average?

For standard deviation, remember that it is based on distances from the mean. Compare:

\[ X_i-M_X \]

with

\[ (X_i+5)-(M_X+5). \]

Simplify the second expression and use that result to explain whether the distances from the mean change.

Question 3 — Research Design and Causation

NoteOriginal question

3. (/1) What fundamental design feature distinguishes a true experimental design from a correlational or quasi-experimental design, and why does this distinction allow researchers to make cause-and-effect claims?

What this question is asking you to do

This is a conceptual question about causal inference. You need to identify the design feature or combination of features that makes a true experiment different from designs in which variables are merely observed or participants are not randomly assigned.

Then you need to explain why that difference matters for cause-and-effect reasoning.

How to approach it

Compare the three designs by asking:

  • Is an independent variable manipulated?
  • Are participants randomly assigned to conditions?
  • Is there a comparison or control condition?
  • Could pre-existing differences between participants explain the result?

A strong answer connects the design feature to the logic of ruling out alternative explanations. Do not stop at saying “experiments can show causation.” Explain how the design helps isolate the effect of the independent variable.

Question 4 — Scales of Measurement

NoteOriginal question

4. (/4) A clinical psychologist evaluates participants entering a treatment program. Identify the scale of measurement used for each of the following variables and briefly justify your answer:

  1. Reaction time in milliseconds to detect an emotional stimulus probe
  2. Total score on the Beck Depression Inventory (ranging from 0 to 63)
  3. Primary psychiatric diagnosis (e.g., Major Depressive Disorder, Generalized Anxiety Disorder, PTSD)
  4. Intake symptom severity rating assessed as Mild, Moderate, or Severe

What this question is asking you to do

For each variable, you must do two things:

  1. identify its measurement scale: nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio;
  2. briefly justify that classification from the properties of the variable.

Do not classify a variable simply because it contains numbers. Ask what those numbers actually mean.

How to approach it

For each variable, work through this decision sequence:

  1. Are the values merely different categories?
  2. Can those categories be placed in a meaningful order?
  3. If the variable is numerical, do equal numerical differences represent equal differences in the measured quantity?
  4. Does zero represent a genuine absence of the measured quantity?

Use those properties in your justification. A good justification is usually one sentence explaining the key feature that determines the scale.

Question 6 — Income Categories

NoteOriginal question

6. (/1) A survey on financial stress asks participants to indicate their annual household income using the categories: Under $30,000, $30,000–$59,999, $60,000–$89,999, $90,000 or more. This variable was measured on a(n) ______ measurement scale.

What this question is asking you to do

You need to classify the recorded variable, not the underlying concept of income in the abstract.

How to approach it

Notice that the survey does not record each person’s exact income. Instead, it places respondents into categories.

Ask:

  • Do the categories have a meaningful order?
  • Do the recorded category labels provide exact, equal numerical distances between respondents?
  • Would arithmetic using category labels preserve the information actually collected?

Use those questions to identify the scale.

Question 7 — Distribution Shape

NoteOriginal question

7. (/1) In a survey measuring weekly video gaming hours among university students, the distribution showed a few people with zero, a large cluster of people in the lowest category (1–2 hours), a moderate amount in the middle (3–5 hours), and only a couple of individuals reporting extreme hours at the very top. In terms of shape, this is an example of a ______ distribution. Draw and label a rough sketch of what this data might look like.

What this question is asking you to do

You need to:

  1. identify the overall shape of the distribution from the verbal description; and
  2. draw a rough graph showing that shape.

How to approach it

Translate the description into a frequency pattern:

  1. Locate where most observations are concentrated.
  2. Locate the few extreme observations.
  3. Decide which side has the longer, thinner tail.
  4. Remember that skew is named for the direction of the tail, not the side containing most observations.
  5. Draw a simple sketch:
    • horizontal axis = weekly gaming hours;
    • vertical axis = frequency;
    • show the large cluster and the sparse extreme values.

9a — Mean

What you need to do

Calculate the average AM score separately for the distracted and not-distracted groups.

How to do it

For each group:

\[ M=\frac{\sum X}{n}. \]

  1. Add all scores in that group.
  2. Count the number of observations.
  3. Divide the sum by \(n\).
  4. Keep the two groups separate throughout the calculation.

9b — Range

What you need to do

Calculate the range of scores separately for each group.

How to do it

For each group:

  1. identify the maximum score;
  2. identify the minimum score;
  3. calculate:

\[ \text{Range}=X_{\max}-X_{\min}. \]

9c — Standard deviation

What you need to do

Calculate the standard deviation separately for each group.

How to do it

For each group:

  1. calculate the mean;
  2. calculate each deviation \(X_i-M\);
  3. square each deviation;
  4. add the squared deviations to obtain \(SS\);
  5. determine whether the course expects the sample or population variance formula for this dataset;
  6. divide by the appropriate denominator;
  7. take the square root of the variance to obtain the standard deviation.

Show enough intermediate work that the calculation can be followed.

9d — Median and mode

What you need to do

Find both the median and the mode for each group.

How to do it

For each group, first sort the scores from smallest to largest.

For the median:

  1. identify the middle position;
  2. apply the appropriate procedure for the number of observations;
  3. follow the course convention where interpolation is required.

For the mode:

  1. count how often each score appears;
  2. identify the most frequent score or scores;
  3. note whether the distribution is unimodal, bimodal, multimodal, or nonmodal if relevant.

9e — 75th percentile

What you need to do

Find the score corresponding to the 75th percentile separately for each group.

How to do it

  1. Order the scores.
  2. Use cumulative frequency to locate the point containing 75% of observations.
  3. Apply the course’s percentile/interpolation procedure, not a different software default.
  4. Remember that the 75th percentile is the third quartile, \(Q_3\).

9f — Compare the two groups

What you need to do

Use the descriptive statistics you have already calculated to discuss how the two groups differ and whether the pattern is consistent with the prediction about distraction.

How to do it

Bring together the results from 9a–9e. Compare:

  • mean;
  • median;
  • mode where informative;
  • range;
  • standard deviation;
  • 75th percentile;
  • any visible differences in the distributions.

Then ask whether these descriptive summaries consistently point in the direction predicted by the study.

Keep the conclusion at the correct level: this assignment provides descriptive statistics. Do not claim statistical significance unless an inferential statistical test has actually been conducted.
